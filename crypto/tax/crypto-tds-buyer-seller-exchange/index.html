1% Crypto TDS in India (2026): Who Deducts It — Buyer, Seller or Exchange?

By Arjun Mehta · Research Editor · How we review

Last reviewed: September 2026

Most Indian crypto users assume the exchange deducts the 1% TDS automatically — and in many centralised trades that is exactly what happens. But “who deducts” is not a fixed rule: it changes with the transaction structure, and it changed again on 1 April 2026. Until 31 March 2026, this rule sat under Section 194S of the Income-tax Act, 1961. From 1 April 2026, the corresponding rule is Section 393(1), Table Sl. No. 8(vi) of the Income-tax Act, 2025. In a direct peer-to-peer deal the buyer deducts. In a crypto-to-crypto swap, both sides can be involved. This guide walks through each scenario so you know whether you are the one who must withhold — because getting it wrong has a real cost for the person who was supposed to deduct but did not.

The short version

Who deducts the 1% TDS, by transaction structure
Transaction structure Who is generally responsible
Direct peer-to-peer deal (not through an exchange) Buyer
Through an exchange, VDA owned by someone else Exchange
Through an exchange, payment routed through a broker Exchange and broker (or broker alone, by written agreement)
Exchange is selling its own VDA Buyer (or buyer’s broker); exchange may take over by agreement
VDA-for-VDA swap (crypto-to-crypto) Both sides; an exchange may handle both legs by agreement
Payment through a payment gateway Gateway not required if the responsible person already deducted

This is educational, not tax advice. The rules depend on your circumstances and can change. Use this to understand the framework, then confirm your own obligations with a qualified professional.

First: TDS is not your tax

Before asking who deducts, it helps to be clear on what TDS is. TDS (Tax Deducted at Source) is a withholding mechanism, not a separate tax. When a qualifying transfer of a Virtual Digital Asset (VDA) takes place, the responsible person deducts 1% of the consideration and deposits it with the government. That amount then shows up as a credit against the relevant person’s final tax liability. It is different from the 30% special rate that taxes income from qualifying VDA transfers. Confusing the two is the single most common source of misunderstanding. (See our full crypto tax guide for that 30% vs 1% distinction.)

The one question that decides who deducts

Under the old Section 194S framework — and its corresponding provision under Section 393(1), Table Sl. No. 8(vi) from 1 April 2026 — TDS generally falls on the person responsible for paying the consideration to a resident seller, subject to the exchange and broker arrangements explained below. So the answer to “who deducts” really reduces to three questions:

  1. Who is paying the consideration?
  2. Is an exchange or a broker involved in the settlement?
  3. Who owns the VDA being sold?

Change any one of those, and the responsible person can change. The scenarios below run through the main combinations set out in the CBDT’s Section 194S guidance. (Income Tax Department)

Scenario 1: a direct peer-to-peer deal

You find a seller directly — outside an exchange’s settlement system — and pay them ₹50,000 for USDT. Here you, the buyer, are generally responsible for deducting 1%, because you are the person paying consideration to a resident seller. The seller’s income tax is a separate matter; the withholding obligation sits with you in this structure.

This is the scenario most beginners get wrong, because they assume “someone else handles it”. In a genuinely direct deal there is no platform in the middle to withhold on your behalf. If you are buying USDT this way, it is worth reading our walkthrough of the routes first. (How to Buy USDT in India)

Scenario 2: a normal centralised exchange trade

You buy or sell crypto on an exchange where the VDA belongs to another user. Here the exchange is generally responsible for deducting TDS at the time it credits the seller or makes the payment. This is why your exchange statement typically shows the TDS being handled for you — but note that this is a withholding arrangement, not evidence that your final tax bill is settled. (See how Binance handles this for Indian users.)

Scenario 3: a broker routes the payment

When a trade happens through an exchange but the payment to the seller is routed through a broker, the position gets more involved: both the exchange and the broker may be responsible, unless there is a written agreement between them that the broker alone will deduct. This is one of the more technical corners of the rule and is exactly why “the exchange always deducts” is not a safe generalisation.

Scenario 4: the exchange itself is the seller

If the VDA being sold is owned by the exchange itself, the responsibility flips: the buyer (or the buyer’s broker) is primarily responsible, although the exchange may take over the obligation through a written agreement with the buyer or broker. Again, the determining variable is ownership of the asset, not the name of the platform. (How platforms differ on TDS handling)

Scenario 5: swapping one crypto for another

Swapping BTC for USDT can feel like you never “cashed out”, but it is still a transfer of a VDA — and so is the other side of the trade. In a VDA-for-VDA exchange, both parties can have withholding obligations, because each is transferring a VDA to the other. Where the swap happens through an exchange, the exchange may deduct on both legs under a written agreement. The rule to remember: “no INR involved” does not mean “no TDS”.

The ₹50,000 and ₹10,000 thresholds

The obligation to deduct is not triggered on every single transaction. It depends on the aggregate consideration during the financial year and on the payer’s category:

  • ₹50,000 — applies to an individual or HUF who falls into the relevant “specified person” category.
  • ₹10,000 — generally applies to other payers.

Because the test is on aggregate consideration across the year rather than each trade in isolation, a long series of small trades can cross the threshold even when no single trade looks large. (Income Tax Department)

The ₹50,000 category in plain terms

The ₹50,000 threshold is not available to everyone — it is reserved for a narrower category that the law labels a “specified person”. In plain terms, that is an individual or HUF who has no income under “profits and gains of business or profession”, or whose previous-year business turnover does not exceed ₹1 crore, or whose professional receipts do not exceed ₹50 lakh. If that is not you, the lower ₹10,000 threshold generally applies instead. The same ₹50,000 and ₹10,000 split carries through the transition to the Income-tax Act, 2025, so the category test continues to apply after 1 April 2026 just as it did under the earlier Section 194S framework. (Income Tax Department)

What you actually have to do if you are the one deducting

If you land on the “buyer must deduct” side of the matrix, you cannot simply ignore it — you have to withhold and deposit. The mechanics split cleanly at 1 April 2026:

  • Before 1 April 2026 (Income-tax Act, 1961) — a specified individual or HUF personally responsible for deducting generally used Form 26QE. Depending on the transaction structure, exchanges could also have reporting obligations under Form 26Q or Form 26QF. (CBDT Circular 13/2022)
  • From 1 April 2026 (Income-tax Act, 2025) — the VDA withholding provision is Section 393(1), Table Sl. No. 8(vi). For an individual/HUF using the challan-cum-statement route, the former Form 26QE has been consolidated into Form No. 141, Schedule D. Certain exchange reporting previously handled through Form 26QF is now reported through Form No. 142. (Form No. 141, Form No. 142)

Form No. 141 is generally due electronically within 30 days from the end of the month in which the deduction is made. This is why a 2026 article should not keep telling every reader “just file Form 26QE” — the correct form depends on whether the transaction falls before or after 1 April 2026, and on your category.

PAN and higher TDS rates

If the deductee does not furnish a valid PAN, a higher TDS rate may apply. For deductions made on or after 1 April 2026, the relevant provision is Section 397(2) of the Income-tax Act, 2025, which replaces the old Section 206AA framework. Older articles may also refer to Section 206AB for non-filers, but that provision was abolished with effect from 1 April 2025 and should not be treated as a current 2026 rule. (Income Tax Act, 2025)

Why this confuses people: “who withholds” vs “who ultimately pays”

The heart of the confusion is that the person who withholds is not necessarily the person who bears the economic cost of the tax. In a P2P deal the buyer withholds, but the seller’s income from the transfer is what ultimately attracts tax. So statements like “the P2P seller pays the 1% TDS” or “the exchange pays your TDS for you” are both imprecise — they blur withholding responsibility with final tax liability. Once you separate those two, most of this article’s matrix becomes intuitive.

A decision rule you can reuse

Rather than memorise the table, run every crypto transaction through three questions:

  1. Who is paying the consideration to a resident seller?
  2. Is an exchange or broker settling or routing the payment?
  3. Who owns the VDA being sold — another user, or the exchange itself?

If the answer is “you, directly, to another person” then assume you may have a withholding obligation and verify it. If an exchange or broker is in the middle, the obligation generally sits with them (or is shared), subject to their written arrangement.

What we cannot generalise

Three areas should not be flattened into a simple rule:

  • Tokenised assets — a blockchain token linked to a share, gold or an ETF does not automatically inherit the tax treatment of the underlying asset; it may or may not be a VDA depending on its structure.
  • DeFi and unusual structures — the Section 194S guidance was written around exchanges, brokers and direct transfers; decentralised and novel arrangements can sit outside the clean scenarios above.
  • The 2026 transition — if the relevant payment or credit occurred on or before 31 March 2026, the Income-tax Act, 1961 framework applies. If the relevant event occurs on or after 1 April 2026, the Income-tax Act, 2025 framework applies. (Income Tax Department)

For these, confirm against current official guidance rather than assuming the matrix applies unchanged.

Bottom line

For most people using a centralised exchange, the platform deducts and deposits the 1% TDS — but only because of how that trade is structured, not because “the exchange always pays it”. The moment you deal directly, or the exchange is the counterparty, or you swap one crypto for another, the responsible person can change — and it can be you. The single most useful habit is to be able to answer, for any transaction: who paid the consideration, who settled it, and who owned the asset being sold.

Frequently asked questions

Who deducts the 1% TDS when I buy crypto on a P2P platform?

In a direct peer-to-peer deal the buyer is generally the person responsible for deducting TDS, because the buyer is the one paying consideration to a resident seller. The platform may take over this responsibility under its own written arrangement, so check what your platform actually states.

Does the exchange always deduct the 1% crypto TDS?

Not always. Where a centralised exchange settles a trade for a VDA it does not own, the exchange is generally responsible. But when the exchange itself is the seller, or when a broker routes the payment, the responsibility can sit with the buyer, the broker, or both, depending on the written arrangement.

Is 1% TDS charged on every crypto transaction?

No. The obligation depends on payer category, the aggregate consideration during the financial year, and the transaction structure. The thresholds are ₹50,000 for an individual/HUF meeting the ₹50,000-category conditions and ₹10,000 for other payers.

Do I pay 1% TDS when I swap BTC for USDT?

A VDA-for-VDA exchange can create withholding obligations on both sides, because each party is transferring a VDA to the other. The absence of INR does not remove the transaction from the VDA TDS framework.

What is the difference between 1% TDS and the 30% crypto tax?

They are different concepts. The 30% rate taxes income from qualifying VDA transfers. The 1% TDS is a deduction at source on qualifying consideration, which acts as a credit against the person’s final tax liability.

If 1% TDS was already deducted, do I still have to pay tax?

TDS is a credit against final liability, not a substitute for it. Your final tax depends on your qualifying VDA income and may still be more than the amount already withheld.

Which form do I use if I am the buyer who has to deduct TDS?

Under the older Section 194S framework, an individual/HUF in the ₹50,000 category generally used Form 26QE. Under the Income Tax Act, 2025 framework, TDS on VDA transfers is handled through Form 141, Schedule D. The correct form and timeline depend on your category and the period, so confirm against current official guidance.
