How to Buy USDT in India with INR: Exchange, P2P & Transfer Routes Compared

Last updated: 6 September 2026 · Rates and fees verified 6 September 2026

There are three ways to turn INR into USDT in India: an Indian exchange with a direct UPI or IMPS deposit, a peer-to-peer (P2P) marketplace like Binance, or a card. They are not interchangeable — they differ in speed, cost and, most importantly, risk. The simplest route is usually an Indian exchange. P2P can sometimes be cheaper, but it adds a markup that varies with the market and a bank-freeze risk that no amount of caution fully removes. And whichever route you choose, the part most people get wrong comes later: selecting the network to withdraw on.

The short version

  • Indian exchange (UPI/IMPS): fastest, usually zero deposit fee, auto TDS, lowest risk.
  • P2P (Binance): no direct INR, a markup that varies with the market, you handle TDS, bank-freeze risk.
  • Cards: possible on some platforms, usually a surcharge on top of the price.
  • Network: use only a network supported by both ends of the transfer; compare fees after confirming compatibility.
  • Tax: 30% on gains plus 1% TDS applies whichever route you use.

Route 1: Indian exchanges — the simplest INR on-ramp

For most people, an FIU-registered Indian exchange is the path of least resistance. You complete KYC once with your PAN, Aadhaar and a linked bank account, then deposit rupees directly.

  • UPI (Google Pay, PhonePe, Paytm) deposits are instant and typically free on platforms like CoinDCX, CoinSwitch, Mudrex and ZebPay.
  • IMPS, NEFT or RTGS handle larger amounts; RTGS is the real-time option for bigger transfers.
  • The exchange deducts 1% TDS automatically, which saves you the paperwork of self-remitting it.

The trade-off: the buy price on an Indian exchange can sit slightly above global spot once you account for the platform's spread or fees, and some banks have been known to block crypto-related UPI transactions. If your bank blocks a deposit, P2P becomes the fallback.

Route 2: P2P — Binance and the markup

Binance does not offer a direct INR deposit, so rupees enter through its P2P marketplace. The flow is simple but worth understanding:

  1. You browse sellers listing USDT for INR and pick one with a good track record.
  2. You pay the seller directly by UPI, IMPS or bank transfer.
  3. Binance holds the seller's crypto in escrow and releases it to you once payment is confirmed.

Two things define the P2P experience:

  • A markup above spot. P2P prices can trade above or below broader market prices, and the premium shifts with the INR–USDT market, the merchant and the payment method. At the time of writing, Binance P2P listings were around ₹102–104 per USDT — check the actual quote at the time of the trade instead of assuming a fixed premium.
  • TDS may be yours to handle. Binance may not auto-deduct TDS the way a domestic exchange does, so the obligation to deduct, deposit and report can fall on you — see our crypto tax guide.

Bank-freeze risk is the part nobody mentions. Indian banks and enforcement agencies have frozen accounts that received P2P inflows traced to fraud — sometimes even for an innocent seller. Trade only with verified merchants (a long history, high completion rate) and consider a separate bank account used only for crypto, so a freeze does not touch your salary or savings.

Route 3: Cards — usually the worst option

Some platforms let you buy USDT with a debit or credit card, but it is rarely the best choice in India. Cards typically carry a surcharge on top of the price, and banks may decline the transaction as high-risk. For most people, UPI or a bank transfer is both cheaper and more reliable. Treat cards as a convenience for occasional small amounts, not a primary route.

Which USDT network to choose

Here is the step where new buyers lose money, and it has nothing to do with price. USDT is not one thing on one chain — the same token is issued across several networks, and you must match the network on both ends of any transfer. Send on the wrong network and the funds may be unrecoverable.

The fee you pay is the exchange's withdrawal fee plus any network cost, and both vary. The most important check is compatibility: confirm both platforms support the same token on the same network before you send.

The right network is the one supported by both the sending and receiving platforms. Compatibility comes first; only then compare fees. Tron (TRC-20) is widely supported and often low-cost, which is why it shows up so often in India, but it is not a universal default — always match the network on both ends. Note that both ERC-20 and BEP-20 addresses start with "0x", so check the network label, not just the address.

What each route actually costs

Cost is more than the headline fee. Add up the parts and the choice usually becomes clearer:

  • Indian exchange: zero or low deposit fee, plus a trading fee or spread (CoinDCX is roughly 0.04–0.2% depending on the product; CoinSwitch prices via a 0–0.49% spread rather than a direct fee). TDS is handled for you.
  • P2P: a markup that varies with the market, often no separate trading fee, but you carry the TDS and the freeze risk.
  • Transfers: the exchange's withdrawal fee plus any network cost, which vary by network and congestion.

And remember: the 30% tax on gains and the 1% TDS apply no matter which route you use. The route changes convenience and risk, not the tax.

How to choose

Choose on two variables: how much simplicity you want, and how comfortable you are with P2P risk.

  • New, small amounts, want it simple: an Indian exchange with a UPI deposit is the right default — fast, cheap and TDS handled for you.
  • Want a wider market or lower spot fees, and accept the trade-offs: Binance P2P, with a verified seller and a dedicated bank account.
  • Withdrawing or moving USDT: pick the network supported by both the sending and receiving platforms, then compare fees.

Mistakes and risks to avoid

  • Choosing the wrong network. Sending USDT on a mismatched network is the most common way to lose funds, and it is usually not reversible.
  • Underestimating the P2P markup. The premium over spot is a real cost, not a rounding error — check the actual INR-per-USDT quote at the time of the trade.
  • Forgetting to self-remit TDS. On P2P the responsibility can fall on you; missing it has consequences.
  • Treating the freeze risk as hypothetical. It has happened to people who did nothing wrong. Use verified merchants and, if you trade regularly, a separate account.

Frequently asked questions

Is buying USDT legal in India?

Crypto is legal in India, though heavily taxed. Buying USDT on an FIU-registered exchange or a P2P marketplace is permitted; the 30% tax and 1% TDS apply to your gains and transfers.

Which is cheaper, an Indian exchange or P2P?

It varies by the moment. P2P can trade above or below exchange prices, so compare the live INR-per-USDT quote including all fees and any markup before deciding.

Why is P2P more expensive than the spot price?

P2P sellers price in convenience, payment risk and their own costs. The premium is the markup you see over the market rate, and it shifts with the INR–USDT market and the payment method.

Can my bank account be frozen from P2P?

It can, if incoming funds are later linked to fraud, even when you acted in good faith. That is why using verified merchants and a dedicated crypto bank account is the standard advice.

Which network should I withdraw USDT on?

Use the network supported by both the sending and receiving platforms, then compare fees after confirming compatibility. Tron (TRC-20) is widely supported and often low-cost; Ethereum (ERC-20) can suit Ethereum-based DeFi. Always match the network on both ends.

Do I pay tax on buying USDT?

Buying itself is not the taxable event, but selling, swapping or otherwise transferring it is. A 30% tax applies to the gain, and 1% TDS applies to qualifying transfers.
