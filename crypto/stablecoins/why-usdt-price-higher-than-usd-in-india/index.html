Why Is USDT More Expensive Than the Dollar in India?

Last updated: 11 September 2026 · Market figures from late-June 2026 reporting; check the live rate before trading

USDT can trade above the dollar in India because demand for dollar-linked crypto liquidity often exceeds the amount of USDT readily available through local INR markets. Bringing additional liquidity into that market is not frictionless: cross-border money movement is subject to foreign-exchange rules, banking controls and compliance risk. The headline 8.5% premium reported in June 2026 was real — but it was calculated against a reference USD/INR rate that ordinary retail users generally cannot transact at directly. So before concluding that USDT is simply "8.5% overpriced", it helps to understand what that number is comparing and what rate you actually pay.

The short version

  • Demand for USDT is persistently strong in India's INR market, partly because stablecoins are the bridge between rupees and other crypto assets.
  • Local USDT liquidity is limited, and the cross-border channels that could add more are constrained by foreign-exchange rules and compliance checks.
  • India's VDA tax and TDS rules can discourage market-making and add friction, which may widen the spread you pay — as can seller and bank-freeze risk.
  • A normal premium is around 3–4%. It widened to more than 8.5% in late June 2026 after the enforcement action in Bengaluru.
  • The headline premium uses the interbank USD/INR rate — your real overpayment against a dollar you can actually obtain is smaller.

First, what the "premium" actually measures

The premium is a simple ratio, but people rarely notice what the denominator is:

Premium = (USDT price in ₹ ÷ USD/INR rate) − 1

In late June 2026, USDT was trading around ₹102.88 on Indian platforms while the reference dollar rate was about ₹94.65. That works out to a premium of roughly 8.7% — the figure quoted across the news that month.

Here is the part most articles skip: ₹94.65 is an interbank or reference rate, not a price you can actually buy dollars at. When you walk into a bank to buy foreign currency, the bank sells dollars at a retail rate a couple of rupees above the reference — often around ₹97 or more, depending on the bank and the day. If your realistic alternative is buying dollars at ₹97, then USDT at ₹102.88 is not 8.7% more expensive than a dollar; it is about 6% more.

So there are really two different questions hiding inside "why is USDT so expensive":

  • Why does USDT trade above the reference dollar rate? This is about India's on-ramp and off-ramp constraints.
  • How much am I actually overpaying? This is about comparing your real route against a dollar you could realistically obtain — and it is a smaller number than the headline.

Why India's USDT is always above the dollar

Three structural reasons keep a permanent gap, independent of any news event.

Demand for USDT is structurally strong

Most international crypto is not quoted directly in rupees, so the usual path is INR → USDT → the coin you actually want. That makes USDT a gateway currency with near-constant demand in India's INR crypto market. Demand is persistently strong partly because stablecoins are commonly used as a bridge between rupees and other crypto assets, which keeps sustained upward pressure on the rupee price.

Local USDT liquidity cannot expand easily

USDT is issued by Tether, so it cannot simply be created to meet local demand. Existing USDT already held in India can circulate between users, but topping up the pool with fresh supply depends on cross-border channels, which are slower and more constrained than a domestic market. When those inflows slow — because of enforcement, banking friction or market-maker caution — the premium rises quickly.

The arbitrage that would fix it is constrained

Normally, if USDT cost ₹102 in India but $1 elsewhere, a trader could buy USDT cheaply offshore and sell it into India, pulling the prices together. In theory, that arbitrage exists. In practice, doing it at scale is far more complicated than ordinary price arbitrage:

  • FEMA and the Liberalised Remittance Scheme currently allow up to USD 250,000 per financial year for permitted transactions, and cross-border remittances by Indian residents have to comply with those rules.
  • Banks and payment providers may apply their own compliance checks to crypto-related transfers, adding delay and cost.

That makes large-scale cross-border crypto arbitrage harder and more expensive, reducing the speed at which price gaps close. India's VDA tax and TDS regime can also reduce the attractiveness and capital efficiency of arbitrage and market-making, making it harder for professional liquidity providers to close the gap quickly. The result is that the normal gap never fully closes: instead of a clean global price, India gets a local price that bounces inside a band.

What lifts it from ~3–4% to more than 8.5%

On top of the structural floor, two things widen the band — and one specific event in 2026 showed both at once.

Tax and seller risk get priced in

These costs and risks can ultimately show up in the price quoted to buyers:

  • The 30% tax on gains, with no loss offset, discourages the professional market-makers who would otherwise hold large USDT inventories and keep the price tight. Thin liquidity means a wider spread.
  • P2P sellers face extra risk — bank accounts can be frozen if incoming funds are later linked to fraud, and honest sellers have faced cybercrime-unit inquiries. That risk is real and gets charged back to the buyer as a markup. (See our guide to buying USDT in India for how that freeze risk plays out in practice.)

The June 2026 enforcement action

On 17 June 2026, the Enforcement Directorate raided six premises in Bengaluru under FEMA, targeting crypto payment firms alleged to have moved more than $265 million (roughly ₹2,500 crore) in unauthorised cross-border transfers using USDT. The immediate effect was a supply shock: market-makers and liquidity providers pulled back from sourcing USDT overseas, and the premium widened from its normal 3–4% to more than 8.5%.

That spike is the clearest demonstration of how the premium works. Nothing changed about USDT's dollar peg or Tether's reserves. What changed was how hard it became to move dollar value in and out of India — and the premium is a live reading of exactly that difficulty.

The number that actually matters: your effective ₹/USDT

Instead of the headline premium, calculate the rate you are actually getting on the route you use:

Effective ₹/USDT = INR you pay ÷ USDT you actually receive

For example, if you pay ₹10,000 and receive about 97 USDT, your effective rate is about ₹103 per USDT — regardless of what headline price the platform advertises. Here is that ₹10,000 across two routes (illustrative figures — check the live quote):

₹10,000 into USDT, two routes compared
Route Approx. price USDT received for ₹10,000 Effective ₹/USDT Premium vs ₹94.65 reference
Indian exchange (spot) ~₹103.00 ~97.1 USDT ~₹103.00 ~8.8%
Illustrative P2P quote ~₹107.00 ~93.5 USDT ~₹107.00 ~13.0%

Numbers are illustrative and move with the market. The point is the method, not the specific digits: divide what you pay by what you receive, then compare that against the reference rate — and, more usefully, against your realistic alternative.

Two things usually surprise people at this step:

  • The exchange and P2P rates are not the same. P2P can sit several rupees above exchange spot, because P2P sellers are pricing in the freeze risk and their own TDS handling that a domestic exchange absorbs for you.
  • Your real overpayment is smaller than the headline. If your honest alternative is a bank retail dollar at ~₹97, an exchange USDT at ₹103 is a ~6% cost, not 8.8%. The premium is real — just make sure you are measuring it against the right baseline.

Does the premium work both ways?

Yes, and this is the part most write-ups leave out. The premium is not a tax on buyers alone; it is a shortage, and a shortage tends to work in the seller's favour too — but not perfectly.

A high local USDT premium can benefit someone selling USDT for INR, but the effect is not perfectly symmetric. P2P buy and sell quotes can differ materially depending on payment method, merchant demand, order size and market conditions. If you hold USDT and want rupees, an elevated premium usually works in your favour — but check both quotes rather than assuming you will capture the full gap.

On an exchange, what you actually capture is the buy/sell spread rather than the headline price — so compare the buy and sell quote for your specific route before you decide, rather than assuming a single "premium" applies to everyone.

How to check the current premium yourself

You do not need to trust anyone's number. It takes about a minute:

  1. Open the live USDT/INR quote on the exchange or P2P marketplace you plan to use.
  2. Look up the current USD/INR reference rate (the RBI reference rate or your bank's forex page).
  3. Divide the USDT price by the USD rate and subtract 1.

Use the reference rate only as a headline gauge. For the number that reflects your cost, compare the USDT price against your actual alternative — the rate your bank would sell you dollars at, or the route you would otherwise use. If the gap between exchange and P2P is unusually wide, that is itself a signal of stress in the P2P market, not a bargain on the exchange. How to reduce the premium you pay

  • Compare exchange vs P2P at the moment of the trade. The gap between them moves; do not assume P2P is always higher or the exchange always fairer.
  • Avoid panic-buying into a spike. Right after a supply shock, prices are at their worst. If you can wait, the premium often cools as supply returns.
  • Factor the whole route, not just the price. A slightly higher exchange rate may beat a lower P2P price once you account for freeze risk and TDS handling — the cheapest sticker price is not always the cheapest total cost.
  • Remember the tax is separate. Whichever route you use, the 30% tax on gains and 1% TDS on qualifying transfers still apply. See our crypto tax guide before you treat the premium as your only cost.
  • Get the basics right first. The route and the price are only half the decision — the network you move USDT on also changes the total cost. Start from our stablecoin guides and the USDT networks comparison.

Frequently asked questions

Is USDT's dollar peg broken when it trades at ₹102 vs ₹94?

No. USDT still trades near $1 against the dollar globally. The gap in India is an on-ramp and off-ramp problem — what it costs to move dollar value in and out of rupees through limited channels — not a failure of the peg.

Why don't traders buy USDT abroad and sell it in India to close the gap?

That arbitrage is what would normally pull the price together, but it is far harder at scale than ordinary price arbitrage. Cross-border remittances have to comply with FEMA and the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, banks may apply their own compliance checks, and the 30% tax on gains with no loss offset plus 1% TDS erodes the profit a professional would make.

Do the 30% tax and 1% TDS explain the premium?

Partly. They add to a seller's cost and discourage the professional market-makers who would otherwise hold large USDT inventories and keep the price tight. They are a real but not the only driver — supply routes and seller risk matter too.

Do Indian USDT sellers also receive the premium?

Yes, a high local premium can benefit sellers, but the effect is not perfectly symmetric. On P2P, buy and sell quotes can differ materially by payment method, merchant demand and order size. On an exchange, the buy/sell spread is what you actually capture, so compare both sides before acting.

What is a normal USDT premium in India?

Historically around 3–4% above the dollar-rupee rate. It widened to more than 8.5% during supply shocks, such as the June 2026 enforcement action in Bengaluru. Check the live rate rather than assuming a fixed number.

Is USDC also at a premium in India?

Usually similar, because the underlying cause is the same: dollar-linked liquidity has to enter through limited INR channels. The two can drift apart at the margin, so compare the live INR quote for each — see our USDT vs USDC comparison — if you are choosing between them.
