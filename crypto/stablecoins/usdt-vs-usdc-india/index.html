USDT vs USDC in India: Which Stablecoin Fits Your Use Case?

Last updated: 6 September 2026

You deposit ₹20,000 on a crypto exchange. The platform offers both USDT and USDC. Both are designed to stay close to one US dollar, both can be transferred on blockchains, and both are widely used as trading and settlement assets. So does it really matter which one you choose?

Yes — but not because one is simply “good” and the other “bad”. The more useful comparison is:

Who issues the token, what supports the $1 value, where can you use it, who can freeze it, what network are you using, and how will you eventually exit?

For an Indian user, there is another important point: a stablecoin may behave like a digital dollar in price, but it is not the same thing as holding US dollars in a bank account. And under India’s current VDA framework, a crypto token can still fall within the virtual-digital-asset rules even if its market price is designed to remain stable. India’s Income Tax Department defines VDAs broadly to include crypto-assets based on cryptographically secured distributed ledgers, while excluding foreign currency itself.[1]

The short answer

For many users, USDT tends to be useful when you care most about exchange liquidity, trading pairs, broad crypto-platform support, and established use for exchange-to-exchange transfers. USDC may be more relevant when you care most about Circle’s reserve disclosure model, native support across many blockchain networks, and integration with regulated financial and payments infrastructure.

But those are tendencies, not permanent rules. Before choosing either one, check: is it supported on your exchange, can you withdraw it, which networks are available, what is the total conversion and withdrawal cost, what will you do with it afterwards, and how will you return to INR?

First: USDT and USDC are not US dollars

This distinction sounds obvious, but it changes how you should think about risk.

USDT

USD₮ is a token issued by Tether. Tether says its tokens are pegged 1:1 with their reference fiat currency and backed by Tether’s reserves. Its current transparency page states that reserve assets exceed liabilities associated with issued tokens.[2]

USDC

USDC is issued by Circle. Circle states that USDC is backed by highly liquid cash and cash-equivalent reserves and is redeemable 1:1 for US dollars through eligible Circle services. Its current reserve disclosure breaks reserves into bank deposits, short-dated US Treasuries and overnight Treasury repo-related holdings.[3]

Neither token is the same as cash sitting directly in your own USD bank account. You rely on an issuer and its reserve/redemption structure.

The crucial word in several rows is platform dependent. Your experience buying USDT on an Indian exchange can be very different from holding USDT in self-custody. The same is true for USDC.

USDT: what actually backs it?

Tether says USD₮ is backed by its reserves and publishes transparency information about tokens in circulation and reserve assets. Its 2026 documentation also states that independent accounting firms prepare reports on quarterly reserve information.[2] That does not mean every USDT holder owns a specific US Treasury bill. A better way to think about it is: USDT is a claim within Tether’s token issuance and reserve framework.

Tether’s terms state that USD₮ is backed by Tether’s reserves, is not fiat itself, and is not protected like an insured bank deposit. Direct issuance and redemption through Tether are also subject to eligibility, verification, minimum amounts and other terms.[4] Buying 100 USDT on an exchange does not mean you personally opened a dollar account with Tether.

USDC: what actually backs it?

Circle’s current reserve disclosures describe USDC reserves as including cash deposits, short-term US Treasury securities and overnight Treasury repurchase arrangements.[3] Circle also says USDC is redeemable 1:1 for USD.

But there is another distinction beginners should understand: holding USDC in a wallet is not the same as having a Circle Mint account. Circle’s own terms distinguish between users who have a Circle Mint account and people who simply hold USDC externally. A wallet holder is not automatically a direct Circle customer with the same account-level services.[5] That means token redemption mechanism and your personal ability to redeem directly with the issuer are two different questions.

“Which one has better reserves?” is too simple a question

A lot of comparison articles stop at “USDC = more transparent, USDT = more liquid”. That skips the decision the user actually has to make. Suppose you want to transfer 500 stablecoins from Exchange A to Exchange B. Even excellent reserve disclosure does not help if Exchange B does not support that stablecoin, supports the token but not your selected network, has withdrawals suspended, charges an unusually high withdrawal fee, or you accidentally use a bridged version. Reserve quality matters, but so does the operational route.

CoinRaahi therefore separates stablecoin risk into four layers:

Issuer → Token → Network → Platform

If you evaluate only the issuer, you are missing three parts of the chain.

Layer 1: issuer risk

USDT depends on Tether; USDC depends on Circle. Questions to ask include: what are the reserves, how are they disclosed, what are the redemption terms, can the issuer restrict addresses, which jurisdiction and regulations apply, and what happens during a banking or liquidity disruption? Neither USDT nor USDC is decentralised in the same sense as Bitcoin. Both depend on central issuers.

Layer 2: token risk

A ticker alone is not enough. A wallet can show USDC or USDT, but you should still verify whether the token is a native issuer-backed token, a bridged representation, an exchange-issued version, or a fake token using the same symbol. This matters especially with USDC. Circle distinguishes native USDC from third-party bridged USDC in its documentation and maintains separate terms for bridged versions.[6] So “USDC on Network X” does not automatically tell you whether Circle directly issued that exact token representation. The contract matters. For how network choices affect a transfer, see our USDT TRC20 vs ERC20 vs BEP20 guide.

Layer 3: network risk

USDT and USDC both operate across multiple blockchains. Tether supports USD₮ on a range of protocols, including Ethereum and TRON. Circle says native USDC was supported on 35 networks as of 29 June 2026.[7] That gives users flexibility, but it also creates risk. A transfer can fail operationally because the sender chooses the wrong network, the recipient does not support that network, the deposit minimum differs, the token contract differs, or the network is temporarily suspended. So the correct order remains: destination support first → network second → fee third.

Layer 4: exchange risk

Suppose you hold 1,000 USDT on an exchange. Even if Tether itself is functioning normally, you can still face platform withdrawal suspension, account review, KYC restriction, exchange insolvency, an unsupported network, or delayed deposit credit. Stablecoin issuer risk and exchange custody risk are separate. Moving USDT into your own wallet removes the exchange custody layer, but it does not remove Tether issuer risk. The same principle applies to USDC and Circle. See our exchange vs self-custody wallet guide for the full custody picture.

Can Tether freeze USDT?

Tether’s current terms explicitly reserve powers to freeze tokens or blacklist addresses in circumstances involving applicable law, prohibited use or other conditions specified in its terms.[4] So self-custody does not mean “nobody can ever restrict this token”. You control the wallet key, but the token contract and issuer still matter. This is fundamentally different from Bitcoin.

Can Circle freeze USDC?

Yes. Circle’s current terms include a blocklisting policy and state that it can block transfers involving certain addresses and freeze associated USDC under specified circumstances, including legal and compliance situations.[5] Again: self-custody ≠ issuer independence. This is one of the most important stablecoin concepts for beginners.

Does that make centralised stablecoins unsafe?

Not automatically. Issuer controls can also support sanctions compliance, fraud response, regulatory obligations, and recovery or enforcement in certain circumstances. But users need to understand that the control exists. The right question is not “can it freeze?”. The better questions are: under what circumstances, who has that authority, and does that fit how I intend to use the token?

Which one is more liquid?

USDT has historically had very deep liquidity across crypto exchanges and a very broad range of trading pairs. That matters if your main use is active crypto trading, moving between exchanges, accessing altcoin markets, or P2P markets. But liquidity is not a permanent property of a ticker. It varies by exchange, trading pair, region, network and market conditions. If your exchange’s USDC/INR or USDC/USDT market is thin, USDC may produce a worse practical execution price even if the token itself is large globally. Always inspect the actual market you will use.

Which one is easier to buy with INR?

There is no permanent universal winner. CoinSwitch currently explains that Indian users can purchase listed stablecoins such as USDT or USDC through its INR buying workflow, while also reminding users that availability differs by exchange.[8] The practical question is therefore: which stablecoin does my chosen platform currently support with the route I need? Check the INR pair, instant-buy quote, trading market, withdrawal availability, supported networks and final INR exit. Do not choose a stablecoin based only on what another exchange offers. For a full INR purchase flow, see how to buy USDT in India.

The ₹10,000 test

Suppose you have ₹10,000 and want a dollar-linked crypto balance. Exchange A offers USDT quote: 119.50 INR per token and USDC quote: 120.10 INR per token. These numbers are only illustrative. The useful comparison is not “both are worth $1, so they cost the same”. Instead calculate:

USDT route

₹10,000 → amount of USDT actually received → withdrawal fee → final amount arriving at destination.

USDC route

₹10,000 → amount of USDC actually received → withdrawal fee → final amount arriving at destination.

Then compare INR spent / stablecoins actually received at destination. That captures spread, trading fee and withdrawal fee. A “better stablecoin” can still be a worse route on a specific exchange.

USDT vs USDC for exchange trading

If your purpose is mainly trading, ask: which one has more pairs, which one has deeper order books, which one is the quote asset for the markets you need, and would converting between the two create another fee or taxable/reportable event? In many crypto venues, USDT remains deeply integrated as a trading quote asset, which can make it operationally convenient. But the answer should come from the actual exchange, not a general rule.

USDT vs USDC for holding in a wallet

If your goal is simply to keep a dollar-linked crypto balance in self-custody, the analysis changes. You need to compare the issuer, reserve framework, chosen network, gas-token requirement, wallet support, smart-contract controls and future off-ramp. Do not stop at “USDC is more transparent”. If you cannot convert your chosen USDC network back to INR efficiently later, that matters.

USDT vs USDC for DeFi

Here, network matters as much as token. Ask: which version is native to the chain, is the token bridged, which protocols accept it, how deep is liquidity, which smart contracts will you interact with, and is there bridge risk? A token can be excellent on one network and awkward on another. For USDC specifically, Circle’s native-versus-bridged distinction is worth checking before depositing into a protocol.[6]

What about earning yield?

This is where users need to be especially careful. USDT and USDC themselves do not magically generate interest merely because they are stablecoins. A yield usually comes from another product layer, such as exchange lending, DeFi lending, liquidity provision, a structured product, or a third-party borrower. So a 7% yield on USDC is not evidence that USDC itself is “paying 7%”. The risk chain becomes:

Stablecoin issuer → Platform/protocol → Borrower/collateral → Smart contract

Do not compare stablecoin safety and yield as if they were the same risk.

A high yield can completely change the risk

Consider Option A: USDC held in a personal wallet. Option B: USDC deposited in an unknown protocol promising 15%. Both balances may still be labelled USDC. But Option B has added smart-contract risk, protocol governance risk, liquidation risk, oracle risk and borrower risk. When comparing returns, identify where the yield comes from. If the answer is unclear, the risk probably is too.

Can USDT or USDC lose the $1 peg?

Yes. “Stablecoin” means the token is designed to maintain a reference value; it does not mean the market price can never move. A depeg can occur because of reserve concerns, banking problems, redemption friction, exchange liquidity, market panic or blockchain disruption. The important question during a depeg is: is this a temporary market discount, or has the redemption mechanism itself become impaired? Those are very different events.

The market price and issuer redemption price are not the same thing

Suppose USDC trades at $0.98 on an exchange during stressed conditions. That does not automatically mean Circle has changed its issuer redemption promise to $0.98. Likewise, a token trading at $1.00 does not prove that every holder can personally redeem directly with the issuer. There are two layers:

Secondary-market price

What buyers and sellers pay on exchanges.

Issuer redemption mechanism

What eligible users can redeem under issuer terms.

Understanding this distinction is more useful than watching a one-minute price chart.

Are USDT and USDC bank deposits?

No. Tether’s terms explicitly state that Tether tokens are not fiat and do not carry the deposit/security protections described in its terms for traditional financial accounts.[4] USDC is likewise a digital token issued under Circle’s terms, not a personal bank deposit simply because reserves include cash and government securities.[5] This distinction should appear in every serious stablecoin guide.

Are stablecoins “foreign currency” for Indian tax purposes?

Do not assume so. India’s current VDA definition specifically excludes foreign currency but separately includes crypto-assets based on distributed-ledger technology.[1] A USD-linked crypto token is therefore not automatically treated as ordinary USD merely because its price tracks the dollar. That is an important conceptual distinction for Indian users.

How Indian VDA tax rules can matter

India’s Income Tax Department currently states that income from transfer of VDAs is subject to the special 30% tax regime under Section 115BBH, plus applicable surcharge and cess, while Section 194S can impose 1% TDS on qualifying VDA transfers subject to applicable thresholds and transaction circumstances.[1] So do not reason “USDT stays at $1, therefore taxes never matter”. The relevant transaction can involve INR → stablecoin, stablecoin → another crypto, stablecoin → INR, stablecoin → another stablecoin, or transfer between wallets. Not all of those actions necessarily have the same tax consequence. For personalised treatment, use professional tax advice. For the general framework, see our crypto tax in India guide.

USDT → USDC is not necessarily “doing nothing”

Suppose you swap 1,000 USDT → 1,000 USDC. Economically, your dollar exposure barely changed. But legally and technically, one crypto asset was disposed of, another was acquired, the issuer changed, the token contracts changed, and potentially the network changed. For Indian record keeping, do not assume that a stablecoin-to-stablecoin conversion can simply be ignored because both are near $1. The Income Tax Department’s current Schedule VDA framework requires VDA transaction reporting on a transaction-wise basis.[9] That does not mean every wallet movement is automatically a taxable disposal; it means users need records capable of distinguishing a transfer from an exchange/disposal.

Wallet-to-wallet transfer is a different question

If you move 500 USDT from your exchange account to your own wallet, you have not automatically changed USDT into another asset merely because the blockchain address changed. But good records still matter. Save the TXID, source, destination, amount, network, withdrawal fee and date. Otherwise, months later your tax history may show an exchange withdrawal without a clear matching destination. See our crypto transfer checklist for what to record.

Which has more networks?

Circle currently says native USDC is supported across 35 blockchain networks as of June 29, 2026.[7] Tether also issues tokens across multiple supported blockchain protocols.[10] But number of networks is not automatically an advantage. A user only needs the networks relevant to their actual route. Ten unsupported networks are less useful than one network supported by your sending exchange, receiving exchange, wallet and DeFi application.

Native vs bridged matters more than most comparison articles admit

Imagine two USDC tokens. Both show USDC. One is native Circle-issued USDC; another is a token created through a bridge structure. They can behave similarly during normal market conditions, but bridge failure introduces another dependency. So your risk chain changes from Circle → USDC → blockchain to something closer to Circle → native USDC → bridge → bridged token → blockchain/application. That extra layer matters. This is why Circle publishes separate information for third-party bridged USDC.[6]

One stablecoin can be safer for one route and worse for another

This is the point most “USDT vs USDC” articles miss. Consider three users.

Priya: active trader

She trades altcoins on an exchange where USDT pairs dominate. For her, USDT liquidity may matter more than having access to every USDC-native chain.

Dev: on-chain user

He uses a protocol on a network where native USDC is deeply integrated. USDC may simplify his route and avoid a bridge.

Neha: beginner saving a small dollar-linked balance

She mostly cares about INR entry, simple withdrawal, clear records and eventual INR exit. For her, the exchange’s actual spread and withdrawal support may matter more than crypto-community arguments about the “better” stablecoin.

Same assets. Different use cases. Different answers.

What about exchange conversions between USDT and USDC?

Some platforms offer a simplified conversion interface. Before using it, check the quoted exchange rate, spread, explicit fee, whether the conversion generates a recorded transaction, and the resulting withdrawal networks. “Zero fee conversion” does not always mean zero economic cost if the quote contains a spread. This follows the same CoinRaahi total-cost principle used for exchange comparisons — see our exchange fees and withdrawals guide.

Do you need USDT or USDC at all?

This is another useful question. If your goal is only to buy Bitcoin and hold it long-term, you may not need to keep a stablecoin balance after the purchase. If your goal is trading, moving dollar-linked value between crypto venues, using DeFi, or settling transactions, stablecoins have a clearer role. Do not hold an extra product just because crypto interfaces make it look like a default step. Every additional asset adds another issuer, transaction, record and potential mistake.

Stablecoins are useful — but the name can create false confidence

The word stable describes the price objective. It does not guarantee issuer solvency, a permanent $1 market price, bank-level insurance, unrestricted redemption, uninterrupted exchange withdrawal, zero smart-contract risk, or zero tax complexity. That is why CoinRaahi treats USDT and USDC as financial infrastructure with specific dependencies, not simply “digital cash”.

The CoinRaahi stablecoin test

Before choosing USDT or USDC, answer these eight questions.

1. Who issues it?

Tether or Circle?

2. What supports the $1 value?

Read the current reserve disclosures.

3. Can the issuer restrict addresses?

Understand the issuer-control model.

4. Which exact token am I holding?

Native or bridged?

5. Which network will I use?

Sender and receiver must match.

6. What will the route cost?

Include spread and withdrawal cost.

7. How will I exit?

Can you efficiently convert it back to INR?

8. How will I document the transaction?

Especially if you trade or swap between VDAs.

If you cannot answer those eight questions, choosing based on the logo is premature.

Bottom line

USDT and USDC solve similar problems, but they are not interchangeable in every situation. USDT may be more convenient when exchange liquidity and crypto-market integration are the priority. USDC may be attractive when Circle’s reserve framework, native multi-chain integrations or particular on-chain applications better match your route. But neither answer should begin with “which stablecoin is safest?”. Begin with “what am I trying to do with it?” Then follow the chain:

INR → Exchange → Stablecoin → Network → Wallet/Application → Exit to INR

At every step, ask what changes. For Indian users, that full route matters more than deciding whether the green USDT logo or blue USDC logo looks more trustworthy.

Frequently asked questions

Is USDC safer than USDT?

There is no universal answer. They have different issuers, reserve frameworks, legal terms, integrations and operational risks. Compare the specific risk you care about rather than using a single “safer” label.

Are USDT and USDC backed by dollars?

Both issuers describe reserve-backed structures linked to the US dollar, but reserve composition and issuer terms differ. Neither should be treated as your personal insured USD bank deposit.

Can USDT and USDC be frozen?

Both issuers have terms allowing address restrictions or freezes under specified legal/compliance circumstances.

Is USDT legal in India?

Avoid reducing the issue to a simple “legal/illegal” label. Crypto-assets operate within India’s VDA tax and reporting framework, while platform access and regulatory obligations are separate questions.

Is USDC treated as USD for Indian tax purposes?

Do not assume so. India’s VDA definition distinguishes crypto-assets from foreign currency. A dollar-linked crypto token is not automatically the same as holding ordinary foreign currency.

Do I pay 30% tax simply for holding USDT?

The special VDA tax regime concerns income arising from the transfer of VDAs, not merely the existence of a wallet balance. The exact tax consequence depends on the transaction.

Is swapping USDT for USDC relevant for tax records?

Potentially. It is an exchange of one crypto asset for another, even if both target $1. Keep transaction records and review the applicable VDA treatment.

Which is better for sending between exchanges?

Whichever has compatible network support, sufficient liquidity and the lower total route cost for the two specific exchanges. Do not choose from the ticker alone.
