Crypto Deposit Confirmed but Not Credited? 6 Things to Check First

Last updated: 14 September 2026 · Deposit requirements vary by exchange and network, and change over time; the deposit page on your exchange shows the number that applies to you

Your transaction says “confirmed” on the block explorer, but your exchange balance still shows zero. The single most useful thing to understand is that confirmed on the blockchain and credited to your account are two different events. A blockchain confirmation means the transaction was successfully recorded on-chain and sent to the destination address. It does not necessarily mean the exchange has recognised and credited it to your account. The first thing to rule out is whether the exchange is simply still waiting on confirmations — in that case the balance appears on its own. If it is not that, there is a specific mistake to identify, and the sooner you identify which one, the better your odds of recovery. The mistake most people make is contacting support with only a screenshot. Work from the transaction ID instead.

The short version

  • Confirm the transaction on a block explorer first: correct address, correct network, and enough confirmations.
  • Rule out the simplest cause first: the exchange is still waiting on its confirmation requirement, and the balance will appear on its own.
  • Before contacting support, rule out the fixable causes: wrong network, missing memo/tag, below the minimum, or sent to the token’s contract address instead of your deposit address.
  • If everything is correct and it still has not credited, contact official support with the full evidence set — not just a screenshot.
  • Never share your seed phrase, password or OTP with anyone claiming to “help” you recover a deposit.

Why a confirmed deposit is not yet in your balance

A deposit passes through three stages, and it helps to keep them separate:

  1. Broadcast — your transaction enters the network’s queue of unconfirmed transactions.
  2. Confirmed — a miner or validator includes it in a block, and later blocks pile on top of it.
  3. Credited — the exchange’s system reads the confirmed transaction, matches it to your deposit address (and memo, if required), and updates your balance.

“Confirmed” means stage 2 finished. The exchange still has its own stage 3 to complete, and it is deliberately more cautious than the network. A transaction with a single confirmation can, in theory, still be reorganised away if the chain briefly splits. So an exchange waits for more confirmations than the network technically needs before it touches your balance. That gap between stage 2 and stage 3 is the reason behind most of this guide.

This is also why you should look at your deposit history page, not just the balance. The history page usually shows the deposit as “processing” or “confirming” even when the balance has not moved yet — a sign that the exchange has already seen your transaction and is waiting on confirmations, which means you usually just have to wait.

Start here: six things to check in order

First: does the deposit already show in your exchange’s deposit history as “processing” or “confirming”? If yes, the exchange has already detected the transaction — do not start troubleshooting the address or network yet. Skip ahead to checking the required confirmations below.

If it does not show at all, run these five checks before anything else, because each one points to a different answer.

What to check when a deposit is confirmed on-chain but not credited
Check What you are looking for If it is wrong
Is the transaction confirmed? The explorer shows success with a growing confirmation count If it is still pending, that is a different problem — wait or check the fee
Is the address correct? The receiving address matches your exchange deposit address exactly A wrong address is usually unrecoverable unless you know who owns it
Is the network correct? The network matches the one your exchange gave you for that asset A wrong network needs support recovery, and it is not guaranteed
Is the memo/tag present? The memo or destination tag is present and matches, if one was required A missing or wrong memo needs support to credit manually
Is the amount above the minimum? The amount meets the deposit minimum shown on the deposit page Below the minimum may not credit automatically

The address and network checks are the ones people rush. An address that starts and ends the same way on an explorer can still be on a different network, because Ethereum and many other chains use addresses that all begin with “0x”.

Case 1: it just needs more confirmations

If the address, network, memo and amount are all correct, the deposit almost certainly just needs time. Each exchange sets its own confirmation requirement per coin and per network, and it changes over time. Rather than guessing a number, check what your exchange actually requires:

What to check, by network, instead of a fixed number
Network What you should check
Bitcoin (BTC) The deposit page may require multiple confirmations
Ethereum (ETH & ERC-20 tokens) The requirement varies by exchange and token
Tron (TRC-20, e.g. USDT) Usually credits quickly once the platform’s required confirmations are reached

Do not rely on a generic confirmation number from another website. The number shown on your exchange’s deposit page is the one that matters.

Larger deposits can also be routed to a manual review, which adds time on top of confirmations.

If the deposit history shows “processing” and the explorer shows a rising confirmation count, the correct action is usually to do nothing for a while — refreshing the balance every thirty seconds does not speed it up. Many platforms suggest waiting roughly 30 to 60 minutes after the on-chain confirmations have clearly passed the deposit page’s requirement before opening a ticket.

Case 2: wrong network — the addresses looked the same

Wrong network is one of the first mistakes to rule out. An EVM-compatible address on Ethereum, BNB Chain, Polygon or Arbitrum can all begin with “0x”, so a pasted address can look correct while being on the wrong network entirely.

The blockchain does not know your intention. It will happily deliver your tokens to an address the exchange controls on a network the exchange may not watch for that asset. What happens next depends on whether the exchange supports that network internally:

  • If the exchange supports the network, recovery is usually possible — often with a fee and after a manual review. Contact support with the transaction ID.
  • If the exchange does not support the network, the funds may be effectively out of reach. This is why the network check must happen before sending, not after.

If you are unsure how one stablecoin can live on several networks, our guide to USDT on TRC-20 vs ERC-20 vs BEP-20 explains the difference and why the cheapest network is not always the right one.

Case 3: a missing memo or destination tag

Some assets — XRP, XLM, EOS, ATOM, HBAR, TON and others — use a shared deposit address at exchanges, and a separate memo or destination tag tells the exchange which individual account the deposit belongs to. If the memo was required and you left it blank or typed it wrong, the transaction can confirm perfectly on-chain while the exchange has no way to route it to you automatically.

The fix is a support ticket, not a wallet action. Provide the transaction ID, the exact amount, the sender and receiving addresses, and the correct memo or tag. Because the funds are identifiable, this is usually recoverable — but it may take time and carry a recovery fee. Do not send the same deposit again until you understand what happened to the first one.

Case 4: below the minimum deposit

Exchanges set a minimum deposit per asset and network, and it exists for a practical reason: crediting a very small deposit can cost the exchange more than the deposit is worth, and tiny amounts are easily swallowed by network fees. If you send below the minimum, the transaction can still confirm on-chain while your account is never credited automatically.

Check the minimum on the deposit page before you send — this matters especially when you are tempted to send a “test transfer” of a tiny amount to a new address. A test that falls below the minimum teaches you nothing useful. If you have already sent below the minimum, contact support with the transaction ID and the amount.

Case 5: you sent to the token’s contract address

This is an under-discussed mistake. On a block explorer, every token has a contract address — the smart contract that implements the token — and it is easy to copy that instead of your own deposit address, because both are long strings shown on the same page. Your deposit address and the token’s contract address are not the same thing.

Sending tokens to the contract address delivers them to the contract itself, and this is often unrecoverable. The issue is not simply whether the contract has an “owner”; many token contracts are not designed with a function that lets accidentally received tokens be withdrawn. This is why the address check is worth an extra ten seconds. Compare the full address from your exchange’s deposit page with the one in the explorer, not just the first and last few characters.

Case 6: deposits suspended, maintenance, or a compliance hold

Sometimes the delay has nothing to do with your transaction. An exchange can temporarily suspend deposits for a specific asset or network during a wallet upgrade, a network hard fork, or congestion. Check the exchange’s status page or the deposit page for any notice. Separately, an account under KYC review or a deposit that triggers a compliance flag can be held until the exchange completes its checks.

If the deposit page itself said “deposits temporarily suspended” when you generated the address, that is the likely cause. The blockchain cannot read an exchange’s maintenance notice; it will confirm your transaction regardless.

What to send support

If you have ruled out the fixable causes and the deposit still has not credited after the confirmation time has passed, contact the exchange’s official support — through the app or website, never through a Telegram or WhatsApp contact. Put everything in one message:

  • Transaction ID (TXID) — the single most useful piece of evidence.
  • Asset and network (for example, USDT on TRC-20).
  • Amount sent, and the time.
  • Sender address and the deposit address you were given.
  • Memo or destination tag, if one was required.
  • Your account UID or email on the exchange.

A complete evidence set usually gets resolved faster than a single “where is my money” message with no transaction ID. Do not include your password, OTP, seed phrase or private key in any message — support does not need them to trace a deposit.

What not to do while you wait

  • Do not resend the same amount to “test” whether the first one arrives — it usually doubles the problem.
  • Do not contact “support” on Telegram, WhatsApp or social media. Real exchange support does not reach out first, and it never asks you to connect a wallet or pay a “recovery fee”.
  • Do not share your seed phrase, private key, password or OTP with anyone, even if they claim to be recovering your deposit.
  • Do not trust a screenshot as proof of a deposit — verify the transaction ID on the explorer yourself.

India users: keep the TXID and original purchase records

For Indian users, it is worth separating two ideas that get tangled up here. A deposit into your own exchange account is a transfer of crypto you already own, not a sale, so it generally does not by itself create a taxable gain. What it does create is a record: the transaction ID, amount, network and date become part of your cost-basis and later reporting trail, which matters when you eventually sell or convert. There is a practical reason to keep it — the receiving exchange may not automatically know your original acquisition cost, so you should keep your own purchase records for later tax reporting.

The India angle also shapes behaviour: if you move USDT on Tron for the lower fees, the wrong-network and missing-memo checks above matter especially. The same deposit checks apply whether you are moving between two exchanges or from a wallet you control. Our crypto tax in India guide covers what happens when you do sell or convert, and the transfers hub has the broader pre-send checklist.

Bottom line: a rule you can reuse

When a deposit shows confirmed but your balance does not move, you are really answering one question: did the exchange fail to credit a correct deposit, or did I send something the exchange cannot credit automatically? If the address, network, memo and amount are all correct, the answer is almost always the first one — wait out the confirmations. If any of them is wrong, the answer is the second one — contact support with the transaction ID, and be honest with yourself about which cases are actually recoverable. A missing memo or tag is often recoverable through support. A wrong-network deposit may be recoverable if the exchange controls the destination address and supports recovery on that network, but recovery is never guaranteed. A wrong address or the token’s contract address is usually not recoverable. That distinction is worth knowing before you send, which is exactly what our crypto transfer checklist walks through, and what the withdrawal side of the same problem looks like when the money is moving the other way.

Frequently asked questions

My deposit shows confirmed on the block explorer but my exchange balance is zero. Is my money lost?

A confirmed transaction does not by itself mean the funds are lost. It means the transfer reached the exchange's deposit address, and the exchange still has to run its own crediting step, which can include waiting for additional confirmations. Run the checks on this page in order: correct address, correct network, memo or tag present, and amount above the minimum. If those are all correct, the deposit normally credits once the confirmation requirement is met; if one is wrong, it usually needs a support ticket with the transaction ID.

How many confirmations does an exchange need before crediting a deposit?

It depends on the network and the exchange, and the requirement changes over time. The number that matters is shown on your exchange's deposit page for that coin and network — do not rely on a generic figure from another website. As a rough guide, Bitcoin deposits can need several confirmations, Ethereum and ERC-20 tokens often need a dozen or more, and Tron TRC-20 typically credits quickly once the platform's required number is reached.

I forgot the memo or destination tag on my deposit. Can the exchange still credit it?

Usually yes, but it will not happen automatically. For assets that use a shared deposit address, such as XRP, XLM, EOS, ATOM, HBAR or TON, the memo or tag is what routes the transfer to your individual account. If it was missing or wrong, the funds can sit uncredited until support identifies the transaction. Contact the exchange with the transaction ID, the exact amount, the sender and receiving addresses, and the correct memo or tag. Recovery may involve a fee and is not guaranteed.

I sent USDT on the wrong network to an exchange. Can I get it back?

It depends. If the funds reached an address the exchange controls and the exchange supports the network you used, recovery is often possible but slow and may carry a fee. If the exchange does not support that network, recovery may not be possible at all. Contact official support with the transaction ID and do not keep resending. The network must match on both ends before you send.

Why does my exchange show a minimum deposit amount?

Exchanges set a minimum because crediting very small deposits costs them more than the deposit is worth, and tiny amounts can be swallowed by network fees. If you send below the stated minimum, the transaction may still confirm on-chain but your account may not be credited automatically. Check the minimum on the deposit page before sending, and if you have already sent below it, contact support with the transaction ID.

Is depositing crypto into my own exchange account a taxable event in India?

Moving crypto you already own into your own account is generally not by itself a sale, so it does not usually create a taxable gain on its own. What matters is keeping the record: the deposit's transaction ID, amount, network and date become part of your cost-basis and later reporting trail. India's tax treatment of virtual digital assets has specific rules, so see our India crypto tax guide or obtain professional advice for your own situation.

Binance or OKX says my deposit is completed, but the balance still has not updated. What should I do?

Treat the exchange's own deposit history as the source of truth, not a generic status label. If the history shows the transfer as processing or confirming, the exchange has detected it and is waiting on its confirmation requirement — give it the required time. If it shows completed but the balance is still wrong, verify the address, network, memo and amount on a block explorer, then contact official support with the transaction ID. Status labels differ by platform, so follow what your own deposit page shows.
