Tokenised Assets: What Backs the Token?

Last updated: 6 September 2026

A tokenised asset is only as good as what stands behind it. A token can represent gold, a share, a bond or property — but the token is the claim, not the asset. To understand what you actually hold, follow the chain from the underlying asset to the issuer, custodian, legal claim, redemption terms and the jurisdiction that would decide a dispute.

The ownership chain

Work through each link before treating a token as if it were the asset:

  1. Underlying asset — what real-world thing is being represented?
  2. Issuer — who created the token and stands behind it?
  3. Custodian — who actually holds the underlying asset?
  4. Legal claim — what right does the token give you?
  5. Redemption — can you exchange the token back for the asset, and how?
  6. Jurisdiction — which laws and courts apply if something goes wrong?
  7. Failure risk — what breaks if any one link fails?

What can be tokenised

Commodities such as gold, equities, bonds, real estate, funds and invoices are all being represented on-chain. The appeal is often described as access, divisibility or settlement efficiency — but the risk profile changes with the structure, so the asset type matters less than the legal wrapper around it.

The questions to ask

Link in the chainQuestion to ask
Underlying assetIs it real, and is it actually held?
IssuerWho is responsible, and are they regulated?
CustodianIs the asset segregated from the issuer’s own assets?
Legal claimDo I own the asset, or only a claim on the issuer?
RedemptionCan I convert back, and under what conditions?
JurisdictionWhich country’s law governs the token?

Redemption

The clearest test of a tokenised asset is redemption: can you exchange the token for the underlying asset, in practice, and not just on paper? A token that can only be sold back on a market, with no redemption route, is a weaker claim than one backed by a defined conversion process.

Jurisdiction and regulation

The issuer’s location decides which regulator and courts you would turn to in a dispute. A token sold globally can still be governed by a single jurisdiction you may have no practical access to. Cross-border structures can make recovery difficult even when the underlying asset is real.

Failure risk

Different links can fail in different ways: the issuer can default, the custodian can fail, a smart contract can have a flaw, or the whole arrangement can be fraudulent. A token that looks like gold can still be a claim on a company that holds no gold. Verification, audit reports and segregation of assets are the things to look for — and none of them remove risk entirely.

Where tokenised assets fit for Indian users

Tokenised assets can offer exposure to global assets with less of the operational friction of some direct routes. But they also introduce a layer of counterparty and jurisdiction risk that a direct holding does not have. The right comparison is not “token vs no exposure” — it is “token vs the direct route”, on the terms that matter to you.

Frequently asked questions

What is a tokenised asset?

A token on a blockchain that represents a claim on a real-world asset such as gold, a stock, a bond or property.

Does a tokenised gold token mean I own physical gold?

It depends on the structure. Some tokens are backed by allocated gold held by a custodian; others are only claims on the issuer.

What happens if the issuer of a tokenised asset fails?

It depends on the legal claim, the backing and the jurisdiction. There can be counterparty risk if the asset is not fully segregated.

How do I evaluate a tokenised asset?

Check the underlying asset, issuer, custodian, legal claim, redemption terms and the jurisdiction that applies.

Are tokenised assets regulated in India?

Treatment can vary by product and jurisdiction. Check current rules before relying on a tokenised asset.
