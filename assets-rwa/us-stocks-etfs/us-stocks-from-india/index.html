How to Invest in US Stocks from India: Routes, Costs, Ownership & Taxes

Last updated: 6 September 2026 · Rules and rates verified 6 September 2026

There are three sensible ways to get US stock exposure from India — and one that is easy to misunderstand. The cleanest route is a direct brokerage under the RBI's LRS scheme (up to $250,000 a year), through a platform like Vested or INDmoney; that gives you direct securities exposure through a regulated brokerage or custody structure. India-listed US funds are simpler but can trade at a premium. And tokenised stocks give you token or contractual exposure whose rights depend on the issuer — a distinction that matters more than the fees. Taxes follow one simple rule: hold over 24 months and the gain is taxed at 12.5%; sell sooner and it is taxed at your slab.

The short version

  • LRS direct brokerage: direct securities exposure via a regulated broker/custodian, up to $250,000/year.
  • India-listed US funds/ETFs: rupees, no LRS paperwork, but often a premium.
  • GIFT City / IFSC: an India-regulated alternative with its own tax treatment.
  • Tokenised stocks: token or contractual exposure; rights depend on the issuer structure.
  • Tax: 12.5% LTCG if held over 24 months, else slab; dividends taxed twice.
  • Report every foreign holding in Schedule FA, even without gains.

Route 1: LRS and a US brokerage — direct securities exposure

This is the standard, RBI-approved way to own US shares directly. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme lets each resident individual remit up to $250,000 per financial year (about ₹2.1 crore) for permitted overseas investments, no special approval needed within that limit.

How it works through a platform like Vested or INDmoney:

  1. Complete KYC with your PAN, Aadhaar or passport, and bank details.
  2. Fill a W-8BEN form — this cuts US dividend withholding from 30% to 25% under the India–US tax treaty.
  3. Remit dollars from your Indian bank under LRS (Form A2, purpose code S0001). Funds usually arrive in 1–3 working days.
  4. Buy US stocks or ETFs, including fractional shares from about $1.

The point worth underlining: here you typically obtain beneficial ownership or a brokerage-held interest in real US securities — with voting rights and dividends — through a US-registered broker, not a derivative of them. The exact custody and fractional-share structure depends on the broker. That ownership is the baseline the other routes are measured against.

Route 2: India-listed US funds and ETFs

If you want rupee-based exposure with no remittance paperwork, India-listed funds that invest in US stocks are the simplest option. You buy and sell them like any Indian mutual fund or ETF, in your existing demat account.

The catch is well documented: when the underlying US market is in demand, these funds can trade at a premium to their net asset value, because SEBI/RBI overseas-investment caps can freeze new supply. That premium is a real cost you pay on top of the fund's expense ratio. This route suits people who want convenience over precision of price.

Route 3: GIFT City / IFSC

GIFT City in Gujarat hosts an International Financial Services Centre where platforms can operate under IFSCA regulation. Some providers — INDmoney among them — use this route to offer US stocks in an India-regulated structure. The tax treatment can differ from a direct overseas brokerage, and there is still nuance over whether resident remittances to GIFT City fall under LRS. This route is newer, so treat the specifics as something to confirm with the platform and your tax professional rather than assume.

Tokenised stocks: what you actually own

Tokenised stocks are the route where the fine print matters most. The idea is that a token tracks the price of a real share — Apple, Tesla, or a whole index — on a blockchain. You get token or contractual exposure, but the rights that back it can be a fully backed token, a contractual claim, or a synthetic or derivative structure, and they are not automatically the same as holding the underlying share.

That difference shows up in three concrete ways:

  • No voting rights, and sometimes no dividends, because you do not hold the actual security.
  • Counterparty risk: the token is only as good as the platform issuing and backing it. If that platform fails, your token's value depends on the platform, not the underlying share.
  • Different regulation: a tokenised exposure is not the same as a demat-held share and may not carry the same protections.

This is not an argument that tokenised stocks are always worse — for small amounts or 24/7 trading they can be useful — but they solve a different problem. If your goal is long-term ownership of a company, the LRS route is the one that actually gives you that. We explore the distinction in more depth in tokenised stocks.

Costs and limits

The visible price is only part of the cost. On the LRS route, add up:

  • FX conversion markup — platforms convert INR to USD at a mid-market rate plus a small margin.
  • Bank wire and platform fees — some platforms charge per remittance or per account.
  • TCS on large remittances — the TCS-free threshold is ₹10 lakh per financial year (from 1 April 2025); above that, 20% TCS is collected on the excess. TCS is refundable/adjustable at filing, not an extra final tax.

On India-listed funds, the costs are the fund's expense ratio plus any premium or discount to NAV. On tokenised exposure, watch for the platform's spread and fees.

Taxes on US stocks for Indian residents

US shares are treated as unlisted securities under Indian tax law, which changes the rules from what you are used to with Indian listed stocks:

  • Holding over 24 months = long-term capital gains, taxed at 12.5% (plus surcharge and cess) with no indexation. There is no ₹1.25 lakh exemption — that applies only to Indian listed shares with securities transaction tax.
  • Holding 24 months or less = short-term capital gains, generally taxed at your income-tax slab rate. Confirm the rate for your assessment year, as it can differ by circumstance.
  • Dividends are taxed twice: the US withholds 25% (with W-8BEN), then India taxes the gross dividend at your slab. You claim a Foreign Tax Credit for the US withholding via Form 67 to avoid paying twice.

The US generally does not tax capital gains of Indian residents on US stock sales, so gains are taxed mainly in India. Gains are computed in INR using prescribed exchange rates, so currency movement itself can change your taxable gain. This is tax information, not advice — confirm the numbers with a professional. Reporting: Schedule FA and FSI

Owning any foreign asset makes ITR filing mandatory, even if your income is below the basic exemption limit. Every year you hold US stocks, you disclose them in Schedule FA and any foreign income in Schedule FSI. Missing this is not a minor omission — non-disclosure can attract penalties under foreign-asset and Black Money provisions. Keep your broker statements and dividend/withholding records, because reconstructing them at filing time is the painful part.

How to choose

  • Want direct, long-term securities ownership: LRS + a US brokerage (Vested, INDmoney or an international broker).
  • Want rupee simplicity and are fine with a premium: India-listed US funds/ETFs.
  • Want token or contractual exposure, in small amounts: tokenised stocks — with eyes open about what you are not holding.
  • Curious about GIFT City: treat it as a case to verify, not a default.

The decision turns on one question first: do you want to own the company, or just its price? Answer that and the route follows.

Frequently asked questions

Is investing in US stocks legal for Indian residents?

Yes, within the RBI's LRS limit of $250,000 per financial year per person. No special approval is needed as long as you stay within it and follow the KYC, W-8BEN and reporting steps.

What is the LRS limit?

$250,000 per financial year for each resident individual, covering all permitted overseas remittances. Above ₹10 lakh in a year, 20% TCS is collected on the excess and is adjustable at filing.

How are US stock gains taxed in India?

As unlisted securities: 12.5% long-term capital gains if held over 24 months, or your slab rate if held 24 months or less. There is no indexation and no ₹1.25 lakh exemption for foreign stocks.

Are dividends from US stocks taxed twice?

The US withholds 25% (with W-8BEN) and India taxes the gross dividend at your slab. You claim a Foreign Tax Credit via Form 67 to offset the US withholding.

Do I have to report US stocks even if I have no gains?

Yes. Foreign holdings must be disclosed in Schedule FA every year, and foreign income in Schedule FSI. Non-disclosure can attract penalties.

What is the difference between owning a share and a tokenised stock?

With direct securities exposure you hold a brokerage-held interest in the security itself — typically with voting rights and dividends. A tokenised stock gives you token or contractual exposure that may not carry the same rights, and it carries the issuing platform's counterparty risk.
