Tokenised Stocks: Same Price, Different Ownership

Last updated: 6 September 2026

A token that tracks Apple’s price is not the same as owning Apple. Same price ≠ same ownership. The difference is not a technicality — it changes what you are entitled to when the company pays a dividend, holds a vote or is involved in a corporate action, and what happens if the issuer of the token fails.

The core distinction

Two things can look identical on a price chart and be different in almost every legal way:

  • Beneficial ownership of the share — you hold an interest in the actual security, typically with voting rights and dividends.
  • A token or contractual claim — you hold an instrument that references the share’s price, with rights defined by the issuer’s terms rather than by company law.

The key question to ask before buying is: what does this token actually entitle me to?

How tokenised stocks are structured

Structures sit on a spectrum:

  • Fully backed: each token corresponds to a real share held by a custodian. The token is meant to be a wrapper around the underlying.
  • Partially backed or fractional: a token represents part of a share, or a basket, held through an intermediary.
  • Synthetic / derivative: the token tracks the price without a matching underlying share. Rights and recourse are defined by contract, not by ownership.

The further a structure moves toward “synthetic”, the more the fine print matters.

Dividends and corporate actions are where the gap shows first: a company can announce a split or a payout, and whether — and how — that reaches a token holder depends on the issuer’s process.

Custody and issuer failure

For a fully backed token, someone must hold the underlying shares. That introduces a custodian. If the custodian, the issuer or the platform fails, what you recover depends on the legal structure and the jurisdiction — which is why “fully backed” is a claim to verify, not a guarantee.

India tax and regulatory considerations

How a tokenised stock is treated in India can depend on whether it is classified as a virtual digital asset, a security or something else, and on the issuer’s regulatory status. Do not assume a token that tracks a US share is treated the same as holding that share. Check current rules and seek professional advice for your own situation.

How to evaluate a tokenised stock

  • Is the token fully backed, and by whom?
  • Who is the issuer, and where is it regulated?
  • Who is the custodian of the underlying?
  • What rights (dividends, voting, redemption) does the token give?
  • What happens if the issuer or custodian fails?

Frequently asked questions

Do I own the underlying share with a tokenised stock?

Not necessarily. You may hold a token or a contractual claim that tracks the price, rather than the security itself.

Do tokenised stocks pay dividends?

It depends on the structure. Some pass dividends through to holders; others do not, because they are not the same as holding the share.

What happens if the issuer of a tokenised stock fails?

It depends on the backing and jurisdiction. If the structure is synthetic or the custodian is affected, there can be counterparty risk.

Are tokenised stocks regulated in India?

Regulatory treatment can vary by product and issuer. Check the current rules and the platform's legal status before relying on one.

What is the difference between a tokenised stock and a real share?

A real share carries ownership rights such as voting and dividends. A tokenised stock may only give price exposure.
