Real Stocks vs Tokenised Stocks: What Do You Actually Own?

Last updated: 6 September 2026

A token linked to Apple, Tesla or Nvidia can move almost exactly like the underlying share. That does not automatically mean you own the same thing. The most important difference is not the blockchain — it is the legal chain between the company, the underlying security and you.

This distinction matters more as tokenised securities move closer to mainstream financial markets. In January 2026, the US Securities and Exchange Commission described tokenised securities as securities represented through crypto networks, while also stressing that different tokenisation models can give holders very different rights. The SEC broadly distinguishes issuer-sponsored tokenisation from products created by third parties.[1]

For an investor, the practical question is therefore not “does this token follow Tesla?”. The more useful question is:

If I buy this product, what legal and economic claim do I actually receive?

The short answer

A real share normally gives you an interest in an actual security held through a brokerage and custody system. A tokenised stock product can take several forms. It may represent an actual security recorded or represented on-chain; a claim backed by shares held by a third party; an entitlement created by a custodian; or a synthetic product designed mainly to reproduce the share price.

Those structures are not equivalent. Two products can both display TSLA $350 while giving the holder completely different rights.

The biggest mistake is assuming the right-hand column is identical for every tokenised stock. It is not.

What do you own when you buy a normal US stock?

Suppose an investor in India buys a US share through an international brokerage. The user normally does not receive a paper certificate with their name on it. Instead, the asset sits inside a regulated securities and custody structure. Depending on the broker, you may hold beneficial ownership of a whole share, a fractional interest, or another securities entitlement through the broker’s custody arrangement. See our guide on how to invest in US stocks from India for the full routes.

That distinction matters, but in each case the product is still built around an actual security, not merely a token tracking the market price. This is why describing all brokerage holdings simply as “the share is registered directly in your name” can also be misleading. In modern securities markets, custody is often layered.

The better questions are: who is the broker, who is the custodian, is the underlying security actually held, are client assets segregated, and what rights flow through to you?

Tokenised stocks can mean very different things

“Tokenised stock” is not one single legal structure. That is probably the most important thing to understand. The SEC’s 2026 framework explicitly recognises different models and notes that tokenised securities can differ in structure and in the rights afforded to holders.[1] In practice, think about tokenised stock products in three broad buckets.

1. Issuer-sponsored tokenised security

This is the closest conceptually to a traditional security. The company or authorised securities infrastructure participates in placing ownership records on-chain. In this model, blockchain technology changes how ownership is recorded or transferred, rather than replacing the legal security with an unrelated token. Tokenisation itself does not necessarily change the underlying legal rights.

2. Third-party backed token

A separate company may buy or custody actual shares and then issue blockchain tokens linked to those shares. The token holder may receive an economic claim connected to the underlying securities. But you still need to ask: are the shares really held 1:1, who holds them, are they segregated, what happens to dividends, can I redeem the token, and what happens if the issuer becomes insolvent? Even if the token is “100% backed”, the investor may be relying on multiple intermediaries.

3. Synthetic or derivative exposure

Some products simply reproduce the economic movement of a stock. Tesla rises 5%, the tokenised product rises roughly 5%. That does not necessarily mean any Tesla share sits behind your position. The product may instead rely on derivatives, collateral, market makers or contractual settlement. This is much closer to price exposure than stock ownership — and that difference becomes critical if something goes wrong.

Same price, different rights

Imagine two products. Product A: you buy 0.1 Tesla share through a regulated brokerage. Product B: you buy a blockchain token called TSLA that tracks Tesla. Both may show the same price movement.

But ask what happens when Tesla pays a dividend, launches a rights issue, splits its shares, holds a shareholder vote, merges with another company or gets acquired. With Product A, your brokerage/custody system normally has procedures for corporate actions. With Product B, everything depends on the token issuer’s contractual rules. That is why price tracking is only one part of ownership.

Dividends are a useful test

One of the fastest ways to understand a tokenised stock structure is to ask what happens when the underlying company pays a dividend. Possible answers include: the holder receives the cash dividend, the issuer converts it into stablecoins, the amount is added to token value, the issuer keeps part as fees, or the product provides no dividend entitlement at all. If you cannot find a clear answer, you probably do not yet understand what you are buying.

Voting rights are another clue

Traditional shareholders can have voting rights, although the practical process depends on how the shares are held. A token with the same ticker does not automatically inherit those rights. Ask whether token holders can vote on directors, acquisitions, shareholder resolutions or other corporate matters. If the answer is no, the product may give you economic exposure without the full rights normally associated with shareholding. Again: same ticker ≠ same ownership.

Who actually holds the underlying shares?

This is one of the most important due-diligence questions. For a backed token, you should be able to identify the token issuer, the underlying securities holder, the custodian, the legal relationship between them, and the rights of the token holder. A vague statement such as “each token is backed by real shares” is not enough on its own. You also need to know whether the underlying securities are segregated from company assets, independently verified, accessible if the issuer fails, and redeemable by the token holder.

“1:1 backed” does not remove counterparty risk

Suppose a provider holds one real share for every token. That is useful information, but there can still be risk between you → token issuer → custodian → underlying share. If any link fails, the legal structure determines what happens next. This is one reason current tokenisation discussions focus heavily on custody, redemption and bankruptcy protection, not merely blockchain technology. Recent submissions around the SEC’s tokenised-securities framework have similarly emphasised backing, custody, disclosure and investor-recovery arrangements.[2]

Blockchain settlement does not automatically make the asset safer

Tokenisation can offer genuine operational advantages, such as longer trading windows, programmable settlement, fractionalisation, easier transfer between compatible systems and faster settlement in some structures. But blockchain introduces another layer of operational risk. Depending on the product, investors may also face smart-contract bugs, wallet mistakes, blockchain outages, unsupported networks, frozen token contracts, bridge risk or loss of private keys. Traditional securities do not normally require retail investors to think about chain compatibility. Tokenised securities can.

Can tokenised stocks trade 24/7?

Sometimes, but “tokenised” does not automatically mean unlimited 24/7 trading. Actual trading hours depend on platform rules, market-maker availability, liquidity, token transfer restrictions, securities laws and settlement infrastructure. A platform can allow token trading outside US stock-market hours while the underlying market itself is closed — which raises another practical question: where does the off-hours price come from? Liquidity and spreads can differ substantially outside normal underlying-market hours.

What if the token issuer disappears?

This is where the legal structure matters most. Ask whether the token itself is legally recognised as the security, whether it is a contractual claim against a company, or whether it is simply a synthetic instrument. These three situations can produce very different outcomes in insolvency. You should look for documentation explaining beneficial ownership, segregation, redemption, bankruptcy treatment and governing jurisdiction. If those details are missing, knowing the blockchain contract address is not enough.

Can you move tokenised stocks to your own wallet?

Some products allow it. Others do not. Even when withdrawal is available, restrictions may apply: whitelisted wallets, identity verification, supported networks, transfer-agent controls, geographic eligibility or smart-contract transfer restrictions. So a product appearing “on-chain” does not automatically mean it behaves like Bitcoin or USDT. Securities can remain permissioned even when blockchain technology is involved.

Tokenised stock versus stock CFD

These are also easy to confuse. A CFD generally gives leveraged or unleveraged contractual exposure to price changes. You do not own the underlying company share. A tokenised product may represent something closer to an underlying security — but only if the legal structure actually says so. Therefore: do not classify a product by the ticker or user interface. Read the product structure. This principle applies to stock tokens, tokenised ETFs, gold tokens and tokenised Treasury products.

Tokenised stock versus perpetual futures

A perpetual contract can also track Tesla, Nvidia or another stock, but it is fundamentally a derivative. It may involve leverage, funding payments, liquidation and margin. That is very different from both a traditional share and a fully backed tokenised security. A user searching “buy Tesla with crypto” could encounter all three — which is why the product name alone is insufficient.

A practical ownership test

Before buying any stock-linked token, answer these eight questions:

  1. What is the legal product? Security, certificate, derivative, contractual claim, or a token representing custody?
  2. Is an actual share held behind it? If yes, who holds it?
  3. Is backing 1:1? And is it independently verifiable?
  4. What happens to dividends? Cash, token, reinvested or nothing?
  5. Do I get voting rights? Do not assume.
  6. Can I redeem the token? For cash, the actual security, or another token?
  7. What happens if the issuer fails? This should be explained before you invest.
  8. Which jurisdiction governs the product? This becomes particularly important for Indian users accessing an offshore platform.

What Indian investors should check

Indian residents have an extra layer to think about. A traditional US security accessed through a regulated investment route and a crypto-based tokenised product may not have the same regulatory treatment, remittance route, tax classification, reporting obligations or custody framework.

Do not assume that because the underlying asset is Apple or Tesla, the Indian tax treatment must be the same as directly holding Apple or Tesla shares. The wrapper matters. For crypto-based products, India’s VDA framework may also become relevant depending on the product structure and facts of the transaction. Traditional shares can also involve intermediary risk

It would be misleading to imply “traditional share = no risk, tokenised share = risk”. Traditional investing also involves broker risk, custody risk, settlement infrastructure, jurisdiction and operational failure. The difference is that the legal and operational frameworks are generally more established. Tokenised products can add new layers: issuer, blockchain, smart contract, wallet and token-specific rights.

So the correct comparison is not safe versus unsafe. It is: what risks exist in each structure, and which party is responsible when something goes wrong?

When tokenisation genuinely adds value

Tokenisation becomes interesting when it improves the market infrastructure without making ownership ambiguous. Possible benefits include fractional access, faster settlement, better collateral mobility, programmable corporate actions, improved interoperability and extended transfer windows.

The strongest long-term model may therefore not be “crypto token that imitates a stock”. It may be the security itself using blockchain infrastructure for part of its official ownership and settlement process. That distinction is becoming increasingly important as regulated markets experiment with tokenised securities infrastructure. In 2026, SEC filings included exchange proposals involving trading securities in tokenised form, showing that tokenisation is increasingly moving into traditional market infrastructure rather than remaining solely an offshore crypto product.[3]

CoinRaahi’s rule: follow the ownership chain

When comparing a traditional asset and an RWA token, do not start with the blockchain. Start here:

Underlying asset → Issuer → Custodian → Legal claim → Token → Investor

For each step, ask: who owes me what? That question usually tells you more than the ticker, blockchain, APY, exchange listing or marketing phrase. This framework is also useful for tokenised gold, tokenised Treasury bills, tokenised funds and real-estate tokens — see our tokenised assets guide for the full chain.

Bottom line

A tokenised Tesla product and a Tesla share can show the same price without being the same asset. Before buying any tokenised stock, verify whether an underlying share exists, who holds it, what legal claim the token represents, whether dividends and voting rights pass through, whether you can redeem, and what happens if the issuer or custodian fails. If those questions cannot be answered clearly, you do not yet know what you own.

Frequently asked questions

Is a tokenised stock a real stock?

Sometimes the token may represent or be backed by a real security, but tokenised-stock structures vary. A token tracking a share price does not automatically give the holder the same legal rights as owning the share through a securities account.

Do tokenised stocks pay dividends?

Some may pass through dividends or equivalent distributions; others may not. Check the issuer's legal and product documentation.

Can tokenised stocks give voting rights?

Possibly, but not automatically. Voting rights depend on how the product is structured.

Are tokenised stocks available 24/7?

Some platforms may offer extended trading or transfer hours, but availability and liquidity depend on the product and venue.

Are tokenised stocks the same as CFDs?

No. CFDs are derivatives. A tokenised product may represent a security, a backed claim or a synthetic exposure depending on its structure.

Are tokenised stocks taxed like US stocks in India?

Do not assume so. Tax treatment can depend on the legal structure of the product and how it is classified under Indian rules.
