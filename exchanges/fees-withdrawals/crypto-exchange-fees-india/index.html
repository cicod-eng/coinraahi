Crypto Exchange Fees in India: What Do You Really Pay?

Last updated: 6 September 2026

An exchange advertises 0.1% trading fee. Another says 0% fee. The second one looks cheaper. It may not be.

For a user starting with ₹10,000, the final cost can also depend on the price quoted when you buy, the spread between buy and sell, whether INR deposits or withdrawals carry a charge, crypto withdrawal fees, the network used, and how much of the asset finally reaches your wallet. This is why comparing exchanges only by the percentage shown on a fee page can be misleading.

The useful question is:

If I start with ₹10,000, how much crypto do I actually end up with after the full route?

The CoinRaahi total-cost model

For most users, think about exchange cost in five parts:

Total Cost = Entry Cost + Trading Cost + Spread + Transfer/Withdrawal Cost + Exit Cost

Not every transaction includes all five. But if you ignore one of them, an exchange can look cheaper than it actually is. This guide is the companion to our fees and withdrawals hub, which maps the same costs across Indian platforms.

1. Entry cost: how does INR reach the platform?

The first cost can appear before you buy any crypto. Depending on the platform and payment route, INR access may involve bank transfer, UPI, P2P, a payment gateway, or another fiat route. Possible costs include an explicit deposit fee, a payment-provider fee, a P2P price premium, transfer limits, and indirect opportunity cost from slow settlement.

A platform can advertise free INR deposit while the actual route still gives you a worse crypto purchase price than another venue. So “free deposit” is only one data point.

2. Trading fee: the easiest number to see

This is the number exchanges most often advertise. A simple example: you buy ₹10,000 worth of crypto. If the trading fee were 0.1%, the fee would be ₹10. That seems small. And for a liquid market with a tight spread, it may indeed be a relatively small part of the total cost. But the trading fee is not always the biggest cost.

3. Spread: the cost beginners often do not see

Suppose Bitcoin’s reference market price is ₹60,00,000, and your platform’s instant-buy quote is ₹60,60,000. That difference is 1%. Even if the app says 0% trading fee, you are still paying an economic cost through the quoted price. This is why zero fee does not mean zero cost.

What is a spread?

At its simplest: spread = the difference between the price at which you can buy and the price at which you can sell. Imagine an app shows Buy USDT: ₹90.50 and Sell USDT: ₹89.00. The ₹1.50 gap matters. If you bought and immediately sold without the market moving, you would already be behind. That loss is not necessarily labelled “trading fee”.

Order book vs instant buy

With order-book trading, you see bids, asks, limit orders and market depth. Costs are often more transparent: you may pay a maker/taker fee, market spread and slippage. With instant buy / convert, the interface may simply say you pay ₹10,000, you receive X USDT. The experience is easier, but the economic cost may be embedded in the quote. For beginners, the best comparison is: how many units do I receive for the same INR amount?

4. Crypto withdrawal fee

Suppose you buy USDT and want to move it to your own wallet. The exchange may charge a withdrawal fee. This fee can be especially important for small balances. Example: you buy 20 USDT and the withdrawal fee is 1 USDT — that is effectively 5% of the balance. Now imagine you buy 2,000 USDT: the same 1 USDT fee is only 0.05%. Same withdrawal fee, very different economic impact. This is why account size matters.

5. Network cost

A withdrawal fee is not necessarily blockchain gas. An exchange can set its own crypto withdrawal fee that may reflect network cost, operational cost, platform policy, batching or internal pricing — but it is not necessarily equal to the exact blockchain fee paid for your transaction. If the exchange charges 1 USDT withdrawal fee, that does not prove the blockchain itself cost exactly 1 USDT.

If you withdraw from your own wallet later, the blockchain network itself may require a native fee asset: Ethereum → ETH, BNB Smart Chain → BNB, TRON → TRX/resource mechanism. Network costs change over time, which is why CoinRaahi avoids publishing permanent claims such as “ERC20 always costs $20” or “TRC20 always costs $1”. A live withdrawal preview is more useful than an old fee table. For how networks affect cost and token identity, see USDT TRC20 vs ERC20 vs BEP20.

6. INR withdrawal and exit cost

Eventually, many Indian users want to move value back to INR. That creates another potential cost layer: crypto → sell → INR → bank. Possible costs include a trading fee, sell-side spread, INR withdrawal fee, payment delay, and tax-related withholding or records. A platform can be cheap to enter and expensive to exit. You should measure both.

The ₹1,000 example

Small users feel fixed costs the most. Suppose Platform A has a trading cost of 0.1%, an effective spread of 0.3%, and a crypto withdrawal fee equivalent to ₹80. For ₹1,000: trading cost ₹1, spread impact approximately ₹3, withdrawal ₹80. Total illustrative friction: ₹84, roughly 8.4% of the starting amount. Notice what happened: the advertised 0.1% fee barely mattered, and the withdrawal cost dominated. This is why a beginner moving very small balances frequently can lose a surprising percentage to operational costs.

The ₹10,000 example

Now use the same illustrative cost structure. ₹10,000 starting balance: trading fee at 0.1% ₹10, spread impact at 0.3% ₹30, withdrawal ₹80. Total ₹120, effective friction 1.2%. Same exchange, same fee structure, but the fixed withdrawal fee matters much less.

The ₹1 lakh example

At ₹1,00,000: trading fee ₹100, spread ₹300, withdrawal ₹80. Total illustrative cost ₹480, effective friction 0.48%. Again, the percentage changes because some costs scale with the transaction while others are fixed.

Why these three examples matter

They show a simple principle: there is no single “exchange fee percentage” that describes every user’s real cost. Your effective cost depends on transaction size, order type, spread, withdrawal behaviour, network, and INR entry and exit route. A platform can be cheap for ₹1 lakh trades and relatively expensive for ₹1,000 transfers.

Do not copy these example percentages into a platform comparison

The numbers above are illustrative. They are designed to show the maths. They are not current Binance, CoinDCX or CoinSwitch fee claims. Platform fees change. For real comparisons, record the live figures on the same date. This distinction should be explicit in every CoinRaahi cost article.

A better way to test an exchange

Instead of comparing home-page claims, run the same route on every platform. For example: ₹10,000 → buy USDT → withdraw to the same wallet/network. Record the INR deposited, USDT quoted, USDT actually purchased, explicit trading fee, withdrawal fee and USDT actually received. Then calculate:

Effective Cost % = (Theoretical Market Value − Final Value Received) ÷ Starting INR

This makes platforms comparable on the user outcome rather than marketing language.

Why the reference price matters

Suppose Exchange A gives you 83.2 USDT and Exchange B gives 82.4 USDT for the same ₹10,000. Before declaring Exchange A cheaper, you need a common benchmark — a major liquid market, a reputable price index, or a same-time market midpoint. Without a reference timestamp, a cost comparison can accidentally measure market movement instead of platform cost. For a serious CoinRaahi test, record date + time + reference price + platform quote. This is much more defensible.

Maker vs taker fees

Many exchanges use a maker/taker model. A maker places an order that adds liquidity to the book (for example, a limit buy below the current ask that waits to fill). A taker is an order that immediately consumes available liquidity (for example, a market buy). Fee schedules can differ. But the lower maker fee is not automatically better if your order never fills, price moves away, or you later chase the market. Execution quality matters as well as fee percentage.

Market order vs limit order

A market order prioritises execution; a limit order prioritises price. For a liquid asset, the difference may be small. For a thin market, a market order can experience slippage. Example: you want to buy ₹1,00,000 of a small token, the order book has limited supply at the displayed price, your order fills across several higher price levels, and the average price becomes worse than the first price you saw. That is slippage.

Spread and slippage are not the same

These terms are often mixed together. Spread is the difference between bid and ask. Slippage is the difference between expected execution price and actual average execution price. A platform can have a tight displayed spread but poor depth for larger orders — meaning ₹1,000 and ₹10 lakh users can experience very different execution quality.

“0% fee” needs four follow-up questions

Whenever you see zero trading fee, ask:

  1. What is the buy quote? Compare with a liquid reference market.
  2. What is the sell quote? Check the spread.
  3. Is there a withdrawal fee? Especially for crypto transfers.
  4. Is zero fee limited? It may apply only to a selected pair, a promotion, maker orders, a VIP tier, or a particular product.

Zero can be real. It just does not automatically mean zero total cost.

P2P has a different cost structure

P2P often advertises no trading fee. But the merchant sets the exchange rate. Suppose the broad USDT/INR market is around ₹90, and a P2P seller quotes ₹92. The effective premium is roughly 2.22%, even if the platform charges no explicit trading fee. That does not mean P2P is always more expensive — the price can vary by payment method, merchant, transaction size, demand and market conditions. The correct comparison is INR paid per USDT actually received, not “P2P fee = zero”.

Payment risk also has a cost

Not every cost appears in rupees. P2P can introduce counterparty risk, payment disputes, bank-account complications, scam risk, and additional TDS/record responsibilities depending on transaction structure. A route that saves ₹30 but creates substantially more operational risk may not be economically superior. Cost analysis should include friction and risk, not only explicit fees.

TDS is not the same as an exchange fee

This is particularly important in India. Under current Section 194S rules, qualifying VDA transfers can be subject to 1% TDS on consideration, subject to applicable thresholds and transaction circumstances. The Income Tax Department currently states thresholds of ₹50,000 for specified persons and ₹10,000 for other payers during the financial year.[1] That 1% is not simply an exchange commission; TDS is a tax-withholding mechanism. The Income Tax Department separately states that income from qualifying VDA transfers is subject to the special 30% regime under Section 115BBH, plus applicable surcharge and cess.[2] So do not calculate trading fee + 1% TDS = exchange fee. They are conceptually different. For the full tax framework, see our crypto tax in India guide.

Why TDS can affect cash flow even if it is not a trading fee

Suppose a qualifying transaction has ₹1,00,000 consideration. A 1% withholding can mean ₹1,000 is deducted/handled under the applicable TDS mechanism. That amount affects immediate cash flow, but it is not the same economic category as a trading fee, spread or withdrawal fee. This distinction matters when comparing platforms. One platform may automate applicable withholding, while another transaction route may create more responsibility for the participants depending on the structure. The Income Tax Department specifically describes different deduction responsibilities for exchange, OTC and VDA-to-VDA transactions.[1]

Do not call TDS “1% tax on every crypto transaction”

That is too crude. Current rules contain threshold conditions, resident/non-resident distinctions, different transaction structures, and specific deductor responsibilities. Use “1% TDS may apply to qualifying VDA transfer consideration under Section 194S”, not “every crypto trade has 1% tax”. This is the wording CoinRaahi should use consistently across the site.

Exchange fees and crypto tax are separate calculations

Imagine you buy for ₹50,000 and later sell for ₹60,000. There are at least three different questions: platform economics (what did the exchange charge?), transaction economics (what spread/slippage occurred?), and tax treatment (what VDA income and withholding rules apply?). Mixing all three into one “fee percentage” produces bad comparisons.

Depositing crypto can also have a hidden cost

Suppose you already hold USDT in a wallet and deposit it into Exchange A. The exchange itself might charge no deposit fee, but getting the USDT there may require network gas, a previous exchange withdrawal fee, or a bridge fee. So free crypto deposit does not mean free to move crypto onto the exchange. The cost may simply occur on the sending side.

Withdrawal availability can matter more than withdrawal fee

Imagine two exchanges. Exchange A has a withdrawal fee of 0.5 USDT but the network you need is suspended. Exchange B has a withdrawal fee of 1 USDT but the network works normally. If you need the funds now, Exchange B may be operationally cheaper despite the larger fee. A fee table without availability can therefore mislead users.

Minimum withdrawal can make a low fee irrelevant

Suppose the withdrawal fee is only 0.2 USDT but the minimum withdrawal is 20 USDT. You hold 10 USDT. The fee looks excellent, but you cannot withdraw. This is why CoinRaahi comparisons should record both withdrawal fee and minimum withdrawal.

Another hidden cost: conversion before withdrawal

Sometimes an exchange supports trading an asset but not withdrawing it. The user may need to sell Asset A, buy Asset B, withdraw Asset B, and potentially swap again later. Now the total route can include two trading events, two spreads, one withdrawal fee, and potentially additional VDA reporting/tax consequences. That can completely change which platform is “cheaper”.

The cheapest exchange depends on what you are doing

Consider four users. User 1 buys ₹1,000 and leaves it on-platform — withdrawal cost barely matters, and spread and small-order pricing matter more. User 2 buys ₹10,000 USDT and immediately withdraws — network/withdrawal fee becomes important. User 3 trades ₹5 lakh regularly — maker/taker tiers, liquidity and slippage matter much more. User 4 mainly needs INR in and INR out — bank routes and sell-side pricing may matter more than crypto withdrawal fees. Same exchange, different economics. See our Binance vs CoinDCX vs CoinSwitch comparison for how these three platforms differ on INR access and withdrawals.

Instead of a single trading-fee column (Exchange A 0.1%, B 0.2%, C 0%), CoinRaahi should use something closer to:

That gives the reader a decision framework rather than one misleading number.

How CoinRaahi should test Binance, CoinDCX and CoinSwitch

For future hands-on comparisons, use the same procedure. Test A — ₹1,000 measures small-user friction. Test B — ₹10,000 measures ordinary beginner use. Test C — ₹1,00,000 measures whether spread and liquidity behave differently at a larger size. For each amount: record the timestamp, record the external reference price, capture the INR quote, record the asset actually received, record the explicit fee, capture the withdrawal preview, record the minimum withdrawal, and — if actually testing transfer — record the final received amount. Do not assume one test amount represents everyone.

Why screenshots matter in fee comparisons

Fee schedules change. A screenshot can preserve the quote, date, fee, network and final amount. If CoinRaahi publishes a hands-on comparison, annotate checked on 6 September 2026 and state platform pricing may have changed since this test. This turns an ordinary SEO comparison into documented first-hand evidence — useful both for readers and for content quality.

What not to do with screenshots

Do not crop so tightly that the date is impossible to establish, the asset/network is hidden, or the platform context disappears. And do not publish UIDs, phone numbers, emails, bank information, or QR codes tied to personal accounts. Evidence should increase trust without exposing private information.

Can two platforms both advertise 0.1% and still cost differently?

Absolutely. Suppose Platform A has fee 0.1% and spread 0.1%, while Platform B has fee 0.1% and spread 0.8%. If every other condition were equal, B is economically more expensive despite the identical advertised fee. This is why fee tables alone are insufficient.

Can the higher-fee exchange actually be cheaper?

Yes. Example: Platform A has trading fee 0.1% and a ₹300-equivalent withdrawal fee; Platform B has trading fee 0.2% and a ₹50-equivalent withdrawal fee. For a ₹10,000 buy followed by immediate withdrawal, Platform A’s explicit illustrative costs are ₹10 trade fee + ₹300 withdrawal = ₹310, while Platform B’s are ₹20 trade fee + ₹50 withdrawal = ₹70. Even before spread, B is cheaper for that route. The trading-fee ranking completely reversed.

The same platform can be cheap for one coin and expensive for another

Withdrawal fees are asset- and network-specific. A platform can be competitive for BTC but less competitive for USDT on a certain network — or vice versa. So there is no defensible long-term statement such as “Exchange X has the cheapest withdrawals”. The correct sentence is: compare the specific asset and network you plan to use.

“Best exchange for India” is usually the wrong question

A more useful question is “best for what?” — best INR access, lowest spread for BTC, cheapest compatible USDT withdrawal, better self-custody support, better tax records, or larger market selection? This is why CoinRaahi comparisons should avoid giving one overall winner unless the criteria are explicit.

The CoinRaahi fee test

Before choosing an exchange, answer:

  1. How will INR enter? And what does it cost?
  2. What is the actual buy quote? Not just the advertised fee.
  3. What is the spread? Compare buy and sell.
  4. How liquid is the market? Especially for larger orders.
  5. Can I withdraw the crypto? And on which network?
  6. What is the withdrawal fee and minimum? Check the live screen.
  7. How will I exit to INR? Measure that route too.
  8. How does the platform handle records and applicable TDS? This matters specifically for Indian users.

If you can answer all eight, you understand the cost much better than someone comparing only 0.1% vs 0.2%.

Bottom line

The headline trading fee is often the least interesting number on a crypto exchange. For a real comparison, measure INR in → asset bought → asset withdrawn or sold → INR/crypto actually received. For small balances, fixed withdrawal fees can dominate. For larger trades, spread, slippage and liquidity can matter more. For Indian users, tax withholding and record keeping are another layer — but TDS should not be confused with an exchange fee. Current Income Tax Department guidance continues to apply a 1% Section 194S withholding mechanism to qualifying VDA transfers subject to the relevant conditions and thresholds, while Section 115BBH separately governs the special taxation of VDA transfer income.[1]

So when an exchange says 0% fee, ask one more question:

How much do I actually end up with?

That is the number that matters.

Frequently asked questions

What fees do crypto exchanges charge in India?

Depending on the platform and transaction, costs can include trading fees, spreads, INR-related charges, crypto withdrawal fees and blockchain/network costs.

Is a 0% trading fee really free?

Not necessarily. The platform may still have a spread, withdrawal fee or other route costs.

What is the difference between spread and trading fee?

The trading fee is an explicit platform charge. Spread is the difference between buy and sell market prices or quotes.

Are crypto withdrawal fees the same as gas fees?

Not necessarily. An exchange can set its own withdrawal fee separately from the underlying blockchain cost.

Why do small crypto purchases feel expensive?

Fixed costs such as withdrawal fees represent a much larger percentage of a small balance.

Is 1% TDS an exchange fee?

No. Section 194S TDS is a tax-withholding mechanism applying to qualifying VDA transfer consideration under specified conditions.

Does every crypto trade in India have 1% TDS?

Do not state it that broadly. The current rules include transaction-specific responsibilities and annual thresholds.

Which crypto exchange is cheapest in India?

There is no universal answer. It depends on the asset, transaction size, spread, withdrawal route and whether you need INR or self-custody.
