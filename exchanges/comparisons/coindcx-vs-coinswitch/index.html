CoinDCX vs CoinSwitch: INR Access, Withdrawals and What You Actually Own

By Arjun Mehta · Research Editor · How we review

Last updated: 25 September 2026 · Withdrawal support and fee figures checked against public CoinDCX and CoinSwitch material on 25 September 2026

CoinDCX and CoinSwitch can look very similar if you only compare the first few steps: both are Indian crypto platforms, both support INR-based buying and selling, and both operate under India’s VDA compliance framework. The bigger difference appears when you try to move your crypto somewhere else. CoinSwitch currently does not support crypto withdrawals. CoinDCX does support withdrawals to eligible external wallets and exchanges, but access is compliance-gated and is not automatically available to every account.

That distinction matters more than whether one app looks simpler or has more trading tools. If your goal is eventually to move BTC, ETH or USDT into your own wallet, the two platforms work very differently. CoinSwitch’s own crypto-deposit page says crypto withdrawals are not currently supported, while CoinDCX says withdrawals can be made to verified and compliant wallets after security and compliance checks.

Quick takeaway: choose the exit route before comparing small differences in fees. If you want the option to move crypto into self-custody, check whether your CoinDCX account is eligible for Crypto Withdrawal access. If you only intend to buy with INR and later sell back to INR, CoinSwitch’s lack of crypto withdrawal may matter less.

The important row is not the deposit row. It is crypto withdrawals.

The biggest difference: can you move the crypto out?

Buying crypto on an exchange and controlling crypto yourself are not the same thing.

While your assets remain inside either CoinDCX or CoinSwitch, you are using a custodial platform. The exchange controls the private keys associated with the underlying wallets, while you control access to your exchange account.

Self-custody only begins once you move the asset into a wallet for which you control the private keys or seed phrase. That is where CoinDCX and CoinSwitch separate.

CoinSwitch: crypto can come in, but it cannot currently go out as crypto

CoinSwitch currently allows eligible crypto deposits from your own wallet or from another exchange after verification. However, its own crypto-deposit page explicitly states that crypto withdrawals are not supported at this time.

That means the normal exit route is:

crypto → sell for INR → withdraw INR to your bank

You cannot currently buy BTC on CoinSwitch and then send that BTC directly to your hardware wallet, a MetaMask-compatible wallet, another crypto exchange or another external blockchain address.

CoinSwitch has previously explained that crypto withdrawal is not a service it currently provides, while users can sell their crypto and withdraw the resulting INR.

This is an important distinction because crypto ownership on an exchange and self-custody are different things. If your long-term plan is simply deposit INR → buy crypto → hold on the platform → sell → withdraw INR, the lack of crypto withdrawal may not change your workflow. But if the plan is deposit INR → buy crypto → transfer to your own wallet, CoinSwitch currently does not complete that route.

CoinDCX: crypto withdrawal exists, but it is compliance-gated

CoinDCX now supports crypto withdrawals to verified and compliant wallets or exchanges. Its current withdrawal flow asks for details including:

  • whether the receiving wallet belongs to you;
  • the blockchain network;
  • the destination wallet address;
  • the withdrawal amount;
  • security verification such as OTP or authenticator confirmation.

A withdrawal may also be paused for additional compliance information. For self-custody wallets, CoinDCX may ask you to prove that you control the destination address — depending on the wallet and account, this can involve a wallet screenshot, message signing or video verification. Once a private wallet has been successfully linked and verified, CoinDCX says it generally does not need to be verified again unless it is disconnected or re-verification becomes necessary.

Important: crypto withdrawal access is not automatic

This is one of the most important details to check before opening an account specifically for self-custody. CoinDCX says Crypto Withdrawal (CW) access is granted only after internal risk and compliance evaluation. Eligible users will see an Opt-In option in the app.

There is also a trade-off that many comparison articles miss: after requesting Crypto Withdrawal access, UPI deposits are disabled for that account. CoinDCX says users with CW access can continue funding through routes such as NEFT or RTGS, but should not assume they can keep the same UPI funding workflow.

So “CoinDCX supports crypto withdrawals” is true, but incomplete. A more accurate description is: CoinDCX offers a route to crypto withdrawal and self-custody for eligible accounts, subject to wallet verification, compliance review and changes to available INR funding methods.

Does CoinDCX control your private wallet after verification?

No. CoinDCX says that connecting or signing a private wallet for ownership verification does not give CoinDCX access to the wallet’s private keys or assets. The signing process is used to prove control of the wallet address, and CoinDCX says it cannot initiate transactions from the connected private wallet or move funds stored there.

That distinction is useful because “verify your wallet” can sound more invasive than it actually is. You are verifying ownership of the address; you are not handing over the wallet seed phrase. Never give an exchange, support agent or anyone else your seed phrase or private key.

INR access: similar goal, different consequences

For an Indian user who simply wants to convert INR into crypto, both platforms are built around domestic INR access. The bigger issue is what happens after the purchase.

On CoinSwitch, the route stays primarily inside an INR-focused loop: INR → crypto → INR. On an eligible CoinDCX account, another route can become available: INR → crypto → verified external wallet. That second route comes with additional compliance checks and may change the INR deposit methods available to you.

This is why comparing the two only on “which app is easier for beginners?” misses the more important question. Ask instead:

Where do I eventually want the asset to end up?

Fees: do not compare one headline number

Fees are another area where overly simple comparison tables can mislead.

CoinDCX fees

CoinDCX uses a tiered trading-fee system. Its current published INR spot schedule ranges by 30-day trading volume; the regular entry tier is currently listed at 0.50% maker and taker, while higher VIP levels receive progressively lower rates. CoinDCX also states that 18% GST applies to fees.

That means there is no meaningful single “CoinDCX fee” without also stating:

  • your trading tier;
  • the market or product;
  • the order type;
  • applicable GST;
  • any withdrawal or blockchain cost.

CoinSwitch fees

CoinSwitch’s own 2026 fee guidance makes an important distinction between explicit trading fees and the bid-ask spread. They are not the same thing. CoinSwitch says the real cost of a spot trade can involve several components:

  • maker or taker trading fees;
  • the bid-ask spread;
  • slippage;
  • deposit or withdrawal-related costs;
  • tax effects.

So it is better not to say that CoinSwitch “hides its fee in the spread.” A more accurate way to compare costs is to look at the actual execution result just before confirming the trade.

What do you actually own on CoinDCX and CoinSwitch?

While your crypto remains on either exchange, the arrangement is custodial. That means the platform controls the private keys; you control an account that records your balance. CoinSwitch itself describes exchange-held crypto as being stored in custodial wallets controlled by the exchange.

This does not mean the balance is imaginary. It means your ability to move or access that asset depends partly on the exchange’s systems, policies and compliance controls. Self-custody is different: with a non-custodial wallet, you hold the seed phrase or private key, and you control blockchain transactions directly. That distinction is why external withdrawal support matters.

What happens if the exchange has a problem?

Neither CoinDCX nor CoinSwitch should be treated like a bank deposit protected by a government deposit-insurance scheme. They use different security and risk-management systems, but those mechanisms should not be confused with a government guarantee.

CoinDCX maintains what it calls a Crypto Investor Protection Fund (CIPF). Its July 2026 transparency report put the fund at approximately ₹42.05 crore — lower than some older published figures, so comparisons quoting approximately ₹55 crore may now be outdated. The fund is a CoinDCX protection mechanism; it is not government deposit insurance.

CoinSwitch describes its custody model as using measures including institutional custody, information-security controls and proof-of-reserves reporting. Those controls can reduce specific risks, but they do not eliminate exchange or counterparty risk. For both platforms, leaving assets on the exchange means you remain dependent on that platform.

TDS: do not confuse TDS with the final crypto tax

India applies TDS to qualifying transfers of virtual digital assets. CoinDCX currently states that for INR trading pairs, TDS is calculated at 1% of the sell-order value. CoinSwitch likewise explains the 1% TDS mechanism for qualifying VDA transfers and notes that the exchange or payer generally deducts it when applicable.

One important 2026 detail is that India’s Income-tax Act, 2025 took effect from 1 April 2026. The familiar VDA rules continue, but some section numbers changed. CoinDCX’s September 2026 tax guide notes that the 1% VDA TDS rule previously associated with Section 194S of the Income-tax Act, 1961 is now referenced under Section 393 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 for transfers from 1 April 2026 onward.

So older articles that still refer only to “Section 194S” may be describing the old numbering even though the underlying 1% mechanism remains relevant. TDS is also not the same thing as your final tax liability — it is tax withheld at source and is generally reflected as a credit when you file your return. For personal tax decisions, verify the current rules with the Income Tax Department or a qualified Indian tax professional. See our India crypto tax guide for the fuller picture.

Does moving crypto to your own wallet create the same issue as selling?

This is where users should be careful with blanket statements. A sale of crypto for INR is clearly different from simply transferring assets between wallets you control. But the documentation, TDS treatment and tax reporting can depend on the nature of the transfer and the parties involved.

For that reason, do not assume that every blockchain withdrawal automatically equals a taxable sale, and do not assume every transfer is tax-neutral without checking the facts. The safest approach is to keep records of:

  • purchase price;
  • date of purchase;
  • transaction ID;
  • destination wallet;
  • proof that you control the destination wallet where relevant;
  • any TDS deducted;
  • eventual sale or swap records.

Which platform fits which workflow?

There is no need to reduce the comparison to “CoinSwitch is for beginners” and “CoinDCX is for traders.” The more useful distinction is the workflow.

CoinSwitch may fit this workflow: you want to deposit INR → buy crypto → keep it on the platform → eventually sell → withdraw INR, and you do not need to send crypto to a personal wallet. Its current lack of crypto withdrawals is much less important in that case.

CoinDCX may fit this workflow: you want to deposit INR → buy crypto → potentially move it to a verified self-custody wallet, and you are willing to go through Crypto Withdrawal eligibility checks, wallet ownership verification, compliance review, possible requests for additional documents, and the loss of UPI deposits after opting into CW access. That last point should be checked before changing your account setup.

A simple decision checklist

Before choosing between the two, answer these questions:

  1. Will I ever want to move the crypto to my own wallet?
  2. Do I specifically need UPI for future INR deposits?
  3. Am I comfortable completing wallet verification and compliance checks?
  4. Will I mostly buy and hold, or trade frequently?
  5. Have I compared the actual trade quote rather than only the advertised fee?
  6. Do I understand the difference between exchange custody and self-custody?

If question 1 is important, withdrawal support should be near the top of the comparison. If question 1 is irrelevant because you intend to remain inside INR in/out, then fees, execution quality and interface may deserve more weight.

CoinDCX vs CoinSwitch: the practical difference

The shortest way to understand the comparison is this:

CoinSwitch currently works primarily as an INR-to-crypto-and-back-to-INR platform. Crypto can be held on the platform, and verified crypto deposits are available, but CoinSwitch currently does not provide an outbound crypto-withdrawal route.

CoinDCX can provide an outbound crypto route, but only for eligible accounts and verified destinations. That route comes with compliance checks, wallet-verification requirements and an important funding trade-off: opting into Crypto Withdrawal access disables UPI deposits.

So do not choose based only on which app appears simpler. Choose based on what you eventually want to do with the asset.

Frequently asked questions

Can I withdraw crypto from CoinSwitch to my own wallet?

No. CoinSwitch currently states that it does not support crypto withdrawals. You can sell crypto for INR and withdraw INR to your linked bank account, but you cannot currently send the crypto directly from CoinSwitch to your own blockchain wallet.

Can I withdraw crypto from CoinDCX to my own wallet?

Potentially, yes. CoinDCX supports crypto withdrawals for accounts that receive Crypto Withdrawal access. The destination wallet must meet its verification and compliance requirements, and additional information may be requested.

Does every CoinDCX account automatically have crypto withdrawals?

No. CoinDCX says Crypto Withdrawal access is granted after internal risk and compliance evaluation. Eligible accounts are shown an Opt-In option in the app.

Can I still use UPI after enabling CoinDCX Crypto Withdrawal access?

CoinDCX currently says UPI deposits are disabled after a user requests Crypto Withdrawal access. NEFT and RTGS remain available according to its help documentation.

Does CoinDCX get access to my private keys when I verify my wallet?

No. CoinDCX says wallet signing is used to confirm ownership of an address and does not give it access to the wallet’s private key, assets or transaction controls.

Which has lower fees, CoinDCX or CoinSwitch?

There is no reliable one-number answer. CoinDCX uses volume-based fee tiers, while CoinSwitch itself advises users to consider trading fees together with spreads, slippage and execution quality. Compare the actual order quote and applicable fee schedule for the product you intend to use.

Are CoinDCX and CoinSwitch custodial?

Yes, while your crypto remains on the exchange, you are relying on a custodial platform rather than controlling the private keys yourself. With CoinDCX, eligible users can eventually move assets to a verified self-custody wallet. CoinSwitch does not currently support that outbound crypto route.

Is CoinDCX’s protection fund government insurance?

No. CoinDCX’s Crypto Investor Protection Fund is a platform-operated protection mechanism. Its July 2026 transparency report listed the fund at approximately ₹42.05 crore. It should not be treated as government-backed deposit insurance.

Is 1% TDS the same as the final crypto tax?

No. TDS is tax withheld at source. Your final tax position depends on your overall taxable VDA activity and applicable Indian tax rules. From 1 April 2026, the new Income-tax Act, 2025 changed the section numbering used for these provisions, so older references to Section 194S should be read with that transition in mind.
